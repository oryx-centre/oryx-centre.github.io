How to check an AI agent’s work

An AI agent's finished output can hide unchecked steps. This guide shows how to check what an agent actually did — its record, its sources, and its decisions — before you trust the result.

In short: an AI agent’s finished output can hide unchecked steps. Check what it actually did — its instruction, its steps in order, its sources, and its decisions — grading each load-bearing item verified, broken, mismatch, or unverifiable, and keeping a one-line log per item.

This guide is a teaching page: it explains how anyone who receives work from an AI agent can check that work, using the same claim-level method Oryx uses in its own published citation audits. It is NOT a certification, NOT a guarantee that following these checks makes an agent’s output correct, and NOT legal or compliance advice. Oryx has not performed verification audits of any client’s AI agent, and it makes no claim about how often agents fail in general — the point of this guide is the check, not a rate. Every audit number below is labelled as a pilot number from Oryx’s own published citation audits.

An AI agent does more than answer a question. It takes steps: it searches, reads sources, calls tools, drafts, revises, and hands back something that looks finished. That finished look is the risk. A chat answer invites you to judge the words on the page. An agent’s output invites you to trust work you never saw happen — the sources it chose, the steps it skipped, the decisions it made without telling you.

Checking an agent’s work means reconstructing what it did from its record, then testing each piece the same way you would test a citation: does the source exist, does it support the claim, and did the agent stay inside what it was asked to do? You need no special tools for this. You need the record, and the willingness to read it.

What counts as the agent’s work

An agent’s work is not just its final answer. It is four things, and a check that looks at only the last one is not a check:

  1. The instruction it was given — what was it actually asked to do, and what limits were set? An agent that was told “summarise these three papers” and then also searched the web has gone beyond its instruction, even if the extra material is good.
  2. The steps it took, in order — what it searched for, what it opened, what tools it used, what it drafted and revised. This is the record, sometimes called a trace or a log. Without it, there is nothing to check against.
  3. The sources it used — every paper, page, document, or dataset its output rests on, with enough detail to find each one again.
  4. The decisions it made — what it included, what it left out, and what it assumed when the instruction was unclear. Assumptions are where agents most often go wrong quietly.

If the agent you used gives you no record of its steps, that is itself a finding — not proof the work is wrong, but a reason to check the output harder, because you cannot verify the process.

The check: four verdicts, six steps

Grade each load-bearing item in the output — each claim, each number, each citation, each action the agent reports taking — with one of four verdicts: verified (the source exists and supports the claim), broken (the source cannot be found), mismatch (the source exists but does not say what the claim says it says), or unverifiable (the source cannot be checked with the tools available — for example, it is behind a paywall you cannot get past, or the agent’s record does not show where the item came from).

Run these six steps on every load-bearing item, or — where the output is long — on a sample that covers the items the rest of the work depends on:

  1. Get the full record. Ask for, or open, the agent’s step-by-step record: what it did, in what order, and what it based each step on. If there is no record, note that and check the output itself more strictly — the absence of a record is a gap, not a pass.
  2. Check each source resolves. Follow every DOI, link, or document reference the output relies on. A dead link is a failed item, not a minor flaw. A citation with no source at all is unverifiable until a source is produced.
  3. Check the source supports the claim. Read the relevant passage, not the abstract alone. Mark it supports, partially supports, or does not support. A real source saying something else is a failed check, not a pass — this is the failure that survives the first step and does the most damage.
  4. Check the agent stayed in scope. Compare what it did against the instruction. Did it use only the sources it was told to use? Did it take actions it was not asked to take — contacting a service, changing a file, adding material from outside the brief? An extra step is not automatically wrong, but it must be visible and justifiable, not discovered afterwards.
  5. Check for invented detail. Names, numbers, dates, and quotations are the items agents most often produce plausibly and wrongly. Cross-check each one in at least two open indexes — Crossref, OpenAlex, Google Scholar, or Semantic Scholar for references; the original document for quoted figures. A perfect-looking reference that appears nowhere is fabricated until proven otherwise.
  6. Record the check. Keep a one-line log per item: the item, the verdict, the date checked, and which source or tool was used. If an item could not be opened, record it as unverifiable — never upgrade it to verified because it sounded right. This log is your defence if anyone later asks how the work was checked, and it is what lets a second person re-check it.

Why this check matters: what the pilot audits found

Oryx’s published audits checked AI-generated citations claim by claim, and the pattern is directly relevant to agent work — an agent’s output is, in large part, a bundle of claims with sources attached. In the larger audit, 194 citations across 30 AI research briefs were checked: 50 verified, 95 broken, 17 mismatched, 31 unverifiable, and 1 with no URL at all (Paper 2 v2, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.23731235 — a pilot, not a universal rate). In a smaller study of 66 AI-generated citations, 31 had broken links, 7 mismatched the claim, 11 could not be verified, and only 5 both resolved and fully supported the claim (Working Paper v3, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.23718472).

Two patterns matter for anyone checking an agent. First, most failures are caught by the first step — following the link. Second, the failures that survive the first step are the dangerous ones: a real source, correctly cited, that does not support the claim attached to it. An agent can produce exactly that failure at scale, fluently, and with complete confidence — which is why the check has to be done by a human reading the source, not by asking the agent whether its own work is correct.

Red flags in an agent’s output

  • No record of steps. The output arrives with no trace of what the agent did. You are being asked to trust the process blind.
  • Sources that do not resolve. Links, DOIs, or document names that lead nowhere. A confident citation to a source that cannot be found is a failed item.
  • Citations that do not support the claim. The source is real; the claim is not what it says. This is a mismatch, and it is the hardest failure to spot without reading.
  • Actions outside the instruction. The agent did things it was not asked to do — extra searches, extra tools, extra material — without flagging them.
  • Confident tone covering gaps. Hedging disappears and uncertainty is presented as fact. An agent that never says “I could not verify this” is one whose unverifiable items are being presented as verified.
  • No named human sign-off. Nobody has accepted responsibility for the output. An agent’s work, like any draft, needs a named human who checked it and stands behind it.

Questions to ask before you trust an agent’s output

Copy these into your own review, or send them to whoever ran the agent:

  1. Can I see the full step-by-step record — what the agent did, in order, and what it based each step on?
  2. For each load-bearing claim, can you point to the exact source and the exact passage that supports it?
  3. Which items could not be verified, and are they marked as unverifiable rather than presented as checked?
  4. Did the agent do anything outside the instruction, and if so, what and why?
  5. Which numbers, names, and quotations were cross-checked against an independent source, and which were taken from the agent’s output as given?
  6. Who is the named human who reviewed this output and accepts responsibility for it?

An output that answers those six questions cleanly is an output whose evidence can be trusted. An output that cannot answer them has told you something important, whether or not any single item turns out to be wrong.

What to do with the result

Sort the checked items into three piles. Accept the items that are verified and in scope — and note that acceptance is yours, as the human reviewer, not the agent’s. Fix and re-check the items that are broken or mismatched: replace the source, correct the claim, or remove the item, then run the check again on the replacement. Reject the output — or send it back — when the load-bearing items fail: if the evidence for the central claims does not hold, the rest of the work cannot stand on it, however polished it looks.

Keep the check record with the output itself, the way you would keep a reviewer’s notes with a manuscript. The next person who reads the work should be able to see what was checked, what the verdicts were, and who signed off — without having to re-run the whole check from scratch. That record is what turns an agent’s output from something you took on trust into something you can defend.

A note on what this check does not do

Checking an agent’s work does not make the work good. An agent can cite real sources accurately, stay in scope, and still produce shallow analysis, a weak method, or a conclusion the evidence does not justify. The check is a floor, not a verdict on quality: it catches invented evidence, unsupported claims, and unrecorded actions before the output is used, and it says nothing about everything else a human reviewer must judge. Treat it as the first screen, applied before the substantive review, not as a substitute for it.
