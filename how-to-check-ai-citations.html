How to check AI-generated citations in your own paper

If an AI tool helped draft your paper, some of its citations may be wrong in ways that look right: a real-sounding author, a plausible journal, a link that leads nowhere or to a paper that says something else. Reviewers and readers will check. Here is how to check first, using a simple four-verdict method and about ten minutes per citation batch.

The four verdicts

Give every citation exactly one verdict:

  • Verified — the link resolves, and the source says what your sentence claims it says.
  • Broken — the link does not resolve, or there is no such paper at all.
  • Mismatch — the source exists, but it does not say what your sentence claims. (This is the most dangerous one: it looks checked.)
  • Unverifiable — you cannot reach the source or cannot confirm what it says, so you cannot stand behind it.

A citation with any verdict other than Verified should not stay in your paper as written. Fix it, replace it, or cut the sentence it supports.

The five-step check

1. Click every link. Not a sample — every link. In a published pilot audit of AI-generated briefs, 47% of citations were broken (66 citations, Paper 2 (2026)). Broken links are the most common failure, and the easiest to catch. A link that does not resolve is a failed citation, full stop.

2. Read the abstract, not just the title. Titles mislead; abstracts decide. Does the abstract actually support your sentence? If you have to stretch the abstract's meaning to fit your sentence, that is a Mismatch. Rewrite the sentence to match the source, or find a source that matches the sentence.

3. Check the details that AI tools invent most often. Year, volume, page numbers, and author lists are the classic fabrication points. Cross-check them against the publisher's page or a scholarly index (Crossref, OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar, or the journal's own site). One wrong detail does not always kill a citation, but it means the rest needs checking too.

4. Ask: does the source support the claim, or just sit near it? A real paper on a related topic is not evidence for your specific claim. This is where most AI-assisted drafts fail quietly — the citation is real, the claim is plausible, and the two have almost nothing to do with each other. If the source does not support the claim, the claim needs its own source or needs to go.

5. Keep a one-line record per citation. Citation, verdict, and the source you actually opened. This takes a minute per citation and saves you twice: first when a reviewer asks, and second when you revisit the paper months later and cannot remember what you checked.

Free tools that help (and what they do not do)

  • Crossref, OpenAlex, and Semantic Scholar: verify that a paper exists and get its real metadata. Free, no account needed for basic lookups.
  • Your library's catalogue or the publisher's site: the final word when an index disagrees with the draft.
  • What no tool does for you: judge whether the source supports your sentence. That judgment is yours, and it is the whole point of the check.

Traps to watch for

  • The confident fake. A complete-looking reference (authors, journal, year, DOI-style string) that resolves to nothing. Always click.
  • The near-miss. A real paper, wrong claim. Always read the abstract.
  • The shifted year or volume. Small errors that signal the reference was generated, not retrieved. Always cross-check metadata.
  • The unverifiable preprint. “Available on request” or a dead preprint server link is not a citation you can defend. Replace it.
  • The borrowed bibliography. A reference list copied from another paper's references without checking that each item supports your text. Every citation in your paper needs your own verdict.

The ten-minute routine (per batch of ~10 citations)

Minutes 1–3: click every link, mark Broken ones. Minutes 4–7: read abstracts for the rest, mark Mismatches. Minutes 8–9: cross-check metadata on any citation that felt off. Minute 10: write the one-line record. Repeat per batch. A paper with 60 citations takes about an hour — less time than answering one reviewer query about a fake reference.
