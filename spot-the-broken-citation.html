Spot the broken citation

Five worked examples, one verdict each. Pick the verdict, then see the answer and the evidence. Free, no sign-up, nothing sent anywhere.

In short: five examples, five verdicts — broken, title mismatch, unverifiable, verified, no URL. These are worked examples constructed to teach the five verdicts found in our published audit of 194 citations; they are illustrative, not quotations from the audited briefs. The real audit’s numbers are on the standing audit page.

Example 1

Claim: “Global AI investment reached $252 billion in 2024.”
Citation: State of AI 2024, Tech Insights Group — link: https://techinsights.example/state-of-ai-2024
What you find: the link returns a 404 page.

What is the verdict?

Example 2

Claim: “Retrieval-augmented generation reduces hallucination by 40% in legal drafting.”
Citation: Patel, R. et al. (2023). Retrieval-Augmented Generation in Legal Drafting. Journal of Legal Technology, 12(3).
What you find: the link opens a real paper — but it is Patel, R. et al. (2023). Retrieval Methods in Contract Review, a different paper, and it reports no 40% figure.

What is the verdict?

Example 3

Claim: “The 2025 benchmark ranks Model X first on reasoning tasks.”
Citation: AI Benchmark Report 2025 — link: https://benchmarks.example/search?q=ai+reasoning+2025
What you find: the link opens a search-results page, not the report. No ranking table can be found from the link as given.

What is the verdict?

Example 4

Claim: “The EU AI Act entered into force on 1 August 2024.”
Citation: Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Official Journal of the European Union — link to the EUR-Lex page.
What you find: the EUR-Lex page opens, shows Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, and states entry into force on 1 August 2024.

What is the verdict?

Example 5

Claim: “Nine in ten enterprises now run at least one AI pilot.”
Citation: Enterprise AI Adoption Survey (2025) — no link, no DOI, no publisher, no page numbers.

What is the verdict?
