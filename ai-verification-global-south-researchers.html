Guide — For researchers

AI verification for Global South researchers

A practical guide to verifying AI-generated research for researchers in the Global South: free tools, open sources, and checks that work without a paid library subscription.

In short: A verification method that works with free tools and open sources only: resolve every DOI, check whether the source supports the claim — not just whether the link works — cross-check in at least two open indexes, and record every verdict. Built for researchers without a big library behind them, including a low-bandwidth routine and a stricter pass for local facts, laws and statistics.

Oryx Centre for Strategic Futures is a two-person research initiative founded 2 October 2026, working on AI governance, geopolitics, emerging technology and strategic foresight for the Global South. It is a Udyam-registered micro enterprise (UDYAM-UP-56-0189301) — NOT a registered organisation and NOT a registered non-profit. This guide is a teaching page: it explains how to verify AI-generated research when you do not have a well-stocked institutional library behind you, using free tools and open sources. It is NOT a certification, NOT a guarantee that following these checks makes a result correct, and NOT legal or compliance advice. The only published audit evidence Oryx has is its citation audits, and every number from them below is labelled as a pilot number. Oryx has not performed verification audits for any client, and it makes no claim about how often AI tools fail on any particular topic or region — the point of this guide is the check, not a rate.

AI tools are now the first draft for researchers everywhere. For a researcher in the Global South, the problem is not just that AI-generated citations can be wrong — it is that the tools for catching the errors are often the same tools you cannot afford. A paywalled journal, a database your institution does not subscribe to, a citation index that barely covers journals from your region: each of these makes the check harder, and each of them is a reason to run the check more carefully, not to skip it.

This guide gives you a verification method that works with free tools and open sources only. A reader who never contacts Oryx should still leave able to do the check.

Why verification is harder — and more important — without a big library

Four structural facts shape the work:

  1. Many sources are paywalled. A citation that points at a paper you cannot open cannot be verified from the citation alone. You need the open version of the paper, or you need to say plainly that you could not check it — and not cite what you could not read.
  2. Institutional access varies. A researcher at a well-funded university in the North usually reaches the same paper through a library proxy. A researcher without that proxy reaches it through open indexes, preprint servers, and the author's own repository — or not at all. The method below is built for the second case.
  3. Citation databases under-cover some journals. Journals published in the Global South are less consistently indexed in the big commercial databases. A paper that does not appear in one database is not necessarily fake — it may simply be outside that database's coverage. That is why the check below uses several open indexes, not one.
  4. AI-generated material about your own region deserves extra care. A model asked to write about a country, a law, or a statistic from a region whose literature is thinly represented in its training data can produce references that look exactly right and are entirely invented. Treat every AI-generated citation about local facts, local law, or local statistics as unverified until you have found the real source yourself.

None of this means the work cannot be verified. It means the verification has to be done with open tools, and the gaps have to be recorded honestly instead of papered over.

The check itself: four verdicts, five steps

Oryx's published method grades each citation with one of four verdicts: verified (the source exists and supports the claim), broken (the source cannot be found), mismatch (the source exists but does not say what the claim says it says), or unverifiable (the source cannot be checked with the tools available — for example, it is behind a paywall you cannot get past). In a published pilot audit of 66 AI-generated citations, 31 (47%) had broken links, 7 (11%) mismatched the claim, 11 (17%) could not be verified, and only 5 both resolved and fully supported the claim (Paper 2, study 1 — a pilot, not a universal rate). In the larger audit behind that paper, 194 citations across 30 AI research briefs were checked: 50 verified, 95 broken, 17 mismatched, 31 unverifiable, and 1 with no URL at all. The lesson is not the percentages. The lesson is that resolving a link and checking support are two different steps, and most failures are caught by the first.

Run these five steps on every AI-generated citation before you submit, cite, or build on it:

  1. Does it resolve? Follow the DOI or link. A dead link is a failed citation, not a minor flaw. If there is no DOI and no link, search the title — see step 4.
  2. Is it the right paper? Title, authors, year, and journal must match — not just resemble. A real paper with the wrong year is a mismatch.
  3. Does it say what you claim? Read the relevant passage, not the abstract alone. Mark it supports, partially supports, or does not support. A real source saying something else is a failed check, not a pass.
  4. Is it real or plausible-looking? Cross-check in at least two open indexes (see the tools below). A perfect-looking reference that appears nowhere is fabricated until proven otherwise — but remember that a paper missing from one index may simply be outside that index's coverage, so check a second one before concluding.
  5. Record the check. Keep a one-line log per citation: citation, verdict, date checked, and which tool you used. If a source was behind a paywall you could not get past, record the verdict as unverifiable — never upgrade it to verified because the title sounded right.

Free tools that work without a subscription

These are all free to use, and none of them needs an institutional login:

  • Crossref DOI resolver — type a DOI in and see whether it exists and what it points to. If the DOI does not resolve, the citation is broken until proven otherwise.
  • OpenAlex — an open bibliographic index. Search by title or author; it also shows whether an open-access version of a paper exists and where.
  • Google Scholar — search the title in quotation marks. It finds papers across publishers, preprint servers, and author pages.
  • Semantic Scholar — an open index with abstracts and, for many papers, free full text.
  • DOAJ (Directory of Open Access Journals) — check whether the journal itself is a listed open-access journal. A journal that is not in DOAJ is not necessarily fake, but a journal that claims to be open access and is absent from DOAJ deserves a second look.
  • Preprint servers and repositories — arXiv, SSRN, and Zenodo for preprints and working papers; the author's university repository for the accepted version. An open version on a preprint server is the same paper you can read and check, even when the journal version is paywalled.

Tools help you find; you still judge support. No tool can tell you whether a paper supports your claim. Only reading the passage can.

When the source is behind a paywall

This is the most common situation for a researcher without a subscription, and it has a clear procedure:

  1. Look for the open version first. Search the title in OpenAlex and Google Scholar and look for a PDF link, a preprint, or an author-repository copy. Many authors post the accepted manuscript legally.
  2. Check the abstract and the metadata — but do not cite from them alone. An abstract tells you the topic, not whether the paper supports your specific claim. If you cannot read the passage your claim rests on, the citation is unverifiable.
  3. Write to the author. Authors usually send a copy when asked, and the request itself is normal academic practice. Allow a few days; do not let a deadline turn an unanswered email into a verified citation.
  4. If you still cannot read it, do not cite it. Replace the claim with one you can source from something you have read, or drop the claim. A citation you could not verify is a risk you are handing to your reviewer, your reader, and your own reputation.

When a paper does not appear in the big databases

A paper from a regional journal may not appear in the index you checked first. Before concluding it is fabricated, try these:

  • Search the journal's own website. Regional journals often publish full text on their own sites, outside the big indexes. If the journal's site lists the article with the same authors, title, and year, that is evidence the paper is real — then check support by reading it.
  • Check regional indexes. SciELO covers journals from Latin America, Spain, Portugal, and South Africa; African Journals Online covers African-published journals. A paper listed there with matching details is a real paper, even if the commercial databases missed it.
  • Check DOAJ. If the journal is listed in DOAJ, its articles are open access and should be reachable from the journal's site.
  • Search the authors. An author with a university page, an ORCID record, or other publications on the same topic is more likely to be real than a name that appears nowhere except the citation itself.

The rule is the same as everywhere else in this guide: a missing result in one place is a reason to check a second place, not a verdict on its own. Two independent open sources confirming the paper's existence is a reasonable bar for "real"; one suspicious-looking result is not.

Checking AI-generated claims about your own country or region

AI tools are at their weakest on facts that are local, recent, or specific: a statistic from a national survey, a clause in a new law, a figure from a ministry report. These are also the claims a reader from your region will check first. Give them a stricter pass:

  • Statistics: verify against the primary source — the national statistics office, the ministry, or the international body that published the number. A secondary source quoting a number is not the number.
  • Law and policy: verify against the official text — the gazette, the parliament's site, or the regulator's site — not against a summary, and not against the model's paraphrase. Laws change; check the version and the date.
  • Local institutions and people: verify names, titles, and dates against the institution's own site. A plausible-sounding official with a slightly wrong title is a common AI invention.
  • Dates: AI tools frequently get the year of a recent event wrong. Check the date of every recent claim against a dated primary source.

If the primary source is not in English, verify in the original language where you can — a translation in the model's output is a second-hand claim, and the check should run on the original.

A low-bandwidth verification routine

If your connection is slow or metered, batch the work instead of checking one citation at a time:

  1. Collect first, check second. Paste every AI-generated citation into one document before you open anything. You will check them in one session instead of many.
  2. Resolve all DOIs in one pass. The Crossref resolver and OpenAlex are lightweight pages; run through the whole list and mark broken ones before you read anything.
  3. Download, then read offline. Where an open PDF exists, download it once and read it offline. Do not re-open the same paywalled page hoping it will load differently.
  4. Check the highest-risk citations first. Citations that carry your central claims, citations about local facts and statistics, and citations with no DOI go first. A citation in a footnote that nothing depends on can wait.
  5. Re-run step 1 on the day you submit. Links break between drafting and submission. A final ten-minute pass over every link catches the ones that died while you were writing.

Common traps

  • A plausible title that does not exist. The most common AI fabrication: a title that sounds exactly like a real paper, with real-sounding authors, in a real journal — and no record of it anywhere. Two open indexes, not one, is the test.
  • A real paper cited for a claim it does not make. The most common failure after broken links. The paper exists, the link works, and the claim is not in it. Check support, not just resolvability.
  • A preprint cited as peer-reviewed work. Preprints are legitimate sources, but they are not peer-reviewed. If your claim rests on a preprint, say so — and check whether a published version exists.
  • A paywalled citation treated as verified. If you could not read it, it is unverifiable. Record it that way, and do not let a deadline promote it.
  • One database treated as the whole literature. A paper missing from one index is not a verdict. Check a second index, the journal's site, and a regional index before concluding anything.
  • A local fact taken from the model. Statistics, laws, and official names about your own country are the claims most worth checking against a primary source — and the ones most likely to be wrong if you do not.
