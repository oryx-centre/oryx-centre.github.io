India AI Governance Guidelines: the seven Sutras, mapped to evidence

MeitY's India AI Governance Guidelines rest on seven Sutras. This guide maps each one to the evidence that shows it is met — who owns it, what record proves it, and what a failed check looks like.

Oryx Centre for Strategic Futures is a two-person research initiative founded 2 October 2026, working on AI governance, geopolitics, emerging technology and strategic foresight for the Global South. It is a Udyam-registered micro enterprise (UDYAM-UP-56-0189301) — NOT a registered organisation and NOT a registered non-profit. This guide is a teaching page: it explains how anyone who deploys, buys, or oversees an AI system in India can test that system against the seven Sutras in MeitY’s India AI Governance Guidelines, using the same claim-level method Oryx uses in its own published citation audits. It is a mapping explainer, NOT legal advice and NOT a compliance certification. Oryx does not certify systems and gives no legal advice. The guidelines themselves are voluntary recommendations, not law. Oryx’s governance mapping work covers the EU AI Act, the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, the OECD AI Principles, and India’s DPDP framework; until now it had no guide for India’s own AI governance guidelines. This guide closes that gap.

India released its own AI governance framework on 5 November 2025: the India AI Governance Guidelines, issued by the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology (MeitY) under the IndiaAI Mission and presented at the India AI Impact Summit in Delhi in February 2026. A drafting committee constituted by MeitY in July 2025 wrote them. The framework is voluntary and principle-based: at this stage, it states, a separate law to regulate AI is not needed, and existing laws — on information technology, data protection, consumer protection, and civil and criminal codes — apply to AI applications.

The framework has four parts: seven guiding principles called Sutras; recommendations under six pillars; a graded action plan with short-, medium-, and long-term steps; and practical guidelines for industry and regulators. The Sutras are the part most often quoted and least often tested. A principle is not a check. This guide turns each Sutra into one: what it asks, who owns it, what record proves it, and what a failed check looks like.

What the guidelines are

The guidelines govern AI applications rather than the underlying technology, through voluntary measures and technical safeguards supported by Digital Public Infrastructure and existing law — a techno-legal approach, not a new AI statute. The seven Sutras are adapted from the Reserve Bank of India’s FREE-AI Committee report on AI in finance, reworked for all sectors. The six pillars — infrastructure, capacity building, policy and regulation, risk mitigation, accountability, and institutions — sit in three domains: enablement, regulation, and oversight. The action plan sets up three new institutions: the AI Governance Group (AIGG), a permanent policy body; the Technology & Policy Expert Committee (TPEC); and the AI Safety Institute (AISI), for technical validation and safety research.

None of that tells you whether a particular system meets the framework. For that you need evidence — a record a second person can read. The check below uses the same four verdicts Oryx uses in its published audits: verified (the record exists and supports the claim), broken (the record cannot be found), mismatch (the record exists but does not say what is claimed), or unverifiable (the record cannot be checked with the means available). A claim about a Sutra with no record behind it is unverifiable, not verified.

The seven Sutras, mapped to evidence

1. Trust is the Foundation

“Without trust, innovation and adoption will stagnate.” Owner: the deployer’s leadership, with the governance group overseeing the system. Record: a published transparency report, an incident log, and a named harms channel. Failed check: no public record of how the system behaves and no one able to say what happens when something goes wrong.

2. People First

“Human-centric design, human oversight, and human empowerment.” Humans must retain meaningful control wherever possible. Owner: the product and design team, with the human overseers named. Record: documented human-in-the-loop points, override logs, and training records. Failed check: no human who can stop the system, no override ever recorded, or users who cannot reach a human when the system affects them.

3. Innovation over Restraint

“All other things being equal, responsible innovation should be prioritised over cautionary restraint.” Owner: the deployer deciding to ship. Record: a written risk assessment, plus pilot or sandbox results. Failed check: deployment with no assessment at all, or a blanket refusal with no reasoning recorded.

4. Fairness & Equity

“Promote inclusive development and avoid discrimination.” Fairness is tested against outcomes, including for marginalised communities. Owner: the data and evaluation team. Record: bias testing across groups, results published, including the worst-performing group. Failed check: no fairness testing at all, testing only on majority groups, or results never shared with the people affected.

5. Accountability

“Clear allocation of responsibility and enforcement of regulations.” Liability is graded by function, risk, and due diligence. Owner: a named accountable person for each system — not a team, not a vendor, a person. Record: a written responsibility allocation, and a grievance log showing complaints received, resolved, and how long each took. Failed check: no named owner, a grievance channel that answers none of its complaints, or a value chain where every party points at another.

6. Understandable by Design

“Provide disclosures and explanations that can be understood by the intended user and regulators.” Owner: the deployer, with the documentation team. Record: a plain-language explanation of decisions, and a regulator disclosure describing how the system works and its impact. Failed check: a user affected by a decision who cannot get an explanation of it, or documentation written only for the vendor’s own engineers.

7. Safety, Resilience & Sustainability

“Safe, secure, and robust systems that are able to withstand systemic shocks and are environmentally sustainable.” The guidelines explicitly encourage light-weight models. Owner: the engineering and safety team. Record: safety testing results, incident-database entries, and sustainability reporting. Failed check: no safety testing before deployment, no incident reporting after it, or no accounting at all for environmental cost.

The six pillars in brief

The Sutras are the philosophy; the six pillars are where the work sits. Infrastructure means expanding access to data, compute, and digital public infrastructure, including AIKosh. Capacity building means education, skilling, and training for citizens, officials, and regulators. Policy and regulation means balanced, agile frameworks, with existing laws reviewed and targeted amendments where gaps are found. Risk mitigation means an India-specific risk assessment framework built on real-world evidence of harm, plus voluntary measures and an AI incidents database. Accountability means the graded liability system and transparency across the value chain. Institutions means a whole-of-government approach, with the AIGG, TPEC, and AISI coordinating, advising, and validating.

The action plan in brief

A deployer claiming alignment today can only be judged against the short-term items. Short-term: establish the AIGG and TPEC; build India-specific risk frameworks; run a regulatory gap analysis; adopt voluntary frameworks; publish a master circular; lay the groundwork for an AI incidents database and grievance redressal; set clear liability regimes; expand infrastructure access; launch awareness and training programmes; and deploy Safe and Trusted tools, including bias mitigation, privacy-enhancing tools, and deepfake detection. Medium-term: publish common standards, operationalise the incidents database, amend laws as needed, pilot regulatory sandboxes in high-risk domains, and integrate digital public infrastructure with AI. Long-term: continuously review the framework, adopt new laws for emerging risks, deepen global engagement on standards, and run horizon-scanning.

How to use the framework as a check

Pick one deployed system and run the seven Sutra checks on it. For each Sutra, write one line: the claim, the verdict, and the record it rests on, with the date. A check done this way is itself evidence: a second person can re-run it from the same records.

There is a reason to be strict. Oryx’s published audits checked AI-generated citations claim by claim, and the pattern is directly relevant — a governance claim is, in large part, a claim with a record attached. In the larger audit, 194 citations across 30 AI research briefs were checked: 50 verified, 95 broken, 17 mismatched, 31 unverifiable, and 1 with no URL at all (Paper 2 v2, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.23731235 — a pilot, not a universal rate). In a smaller study of 66 AI-generated citations, 31 had broken links, 7 mismatched the claim, 11 could not be verified, and only 5 both resolved and fully supported the claim (Working Paper v3, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.23718472). Most failures are caught by the first step — asking for the record. The dangerous failures survive it: a real record, correctly cited, that does not support the claim attached to it. That is the mismatch — what a governance self-assessment produces when nobody reads the record.

Red flags when a system claims alignment

  • No record offered. The claim rests on a slide deck, not on a transparency report, a test result, or a log.
  • The Sutras quoted, never tested. All seven named in a proposal, none mapped to a record or an owner.
  • Fairness claimed without a test. No bias testing, or testing that never looked at marginalised communities.
  • Accountability with no name. Responsibility sits with a team, a vendor, or a committee — never a person.
  • No incident channel. Nowhere for a harmed user to report, or a channel where nothing is ever resolved.
  • “Compliant” used loosely. The guidelines are voluntary recommendations. A system described as compliant is using a word the framework does not support — ask what it complies with, and under which law.

Questions to ask before you accept an alignment claim

Copy these into your own review, or send them to whoever claims the system is aligned:

  1. For each Sutra, who owns it — a named person — and what record proves it?
  2. Where is the transparency report on the risk of harm, and when was it updated?
  3. Which groups were included in fairness testing, and where are the results, including the worst-performing group?
  4. How does a user affected by a decision get an explanation of it, in language they can understand?
  5. Where is the grievance redressal mechanism, and how many complaints has it resolved, in what time?
  6. What safety testing was done before deployment, and where are incidents reported now?
  7. Which action-plan items — short-, medium-, or long-term — does it claim, and what is the dated evidence?

A system that answers those seven questions cleanly is one whose alignment can be checked. A system that cannot answer them has told you something important, whether or not any single Sutra turns out to be violated.

What to do with the result

Sort the seven checks into three piles. Accept the Sutras that are verified, with the record attached — acceptance is yours, as the human reviewer, not the vendor’s. Fix and re-check the Sutras that are broken or mismatched: produce the record, correct the claim, or change the system, then check again. Reject the alignment claim when the load-bearing Sutras fail: if accountability has no name and fairness has no test, the rest cannot stand on it, however completely the proposal quotes the Sutras. Keep the check record with the procurement or deployment file, so the next reader can see what was checked and who signed off.

A note on what this guide does not do

Mapping the Sutras to evidence does not make a system good, lawful, or safe. A system can have a named owner for every Sutra and a published transparency report and still produce weak analysis — and this guide says nothing about any legal obligation, because the guidelines are recommendations, not law. The check is a floor, not a verdict on quality: it catches alignment claims with no record behind them, and says nothing about everything else a human reviewer, a regulator, or a court must judge. Treat it as the first screen, before the substantive review, not as a substitute for it.
