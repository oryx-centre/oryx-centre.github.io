Framing

Sub-questions

  1. What is the prevalence and nature of AI-generated hallucinations in academic citations?
    • Evidence required: Peer-reviewed studies, technical reports, or systematic analyses documenting instances where Large Language Models (LLMs) have fabricated citations (e.g., non-existent papers, incorrect authors, or mismatched titles).
  2. How are academic publishers and institutions responding to the detection of AI-generated citations?
    • Evidence required: Official policy statements, editorial guidelines, and reports on the implementation of detection tools or screening processes by major academic journals and universities.
  3. What are the ethical and integrity implications of AI-generated citations in scholarly communication?
    • Evidence required: Commentary, ethics committee reports, or editorial board discussions regarding the impact on trust, reproducibility, and the scholarly record.
  4. What technical methods are currently being used to identify or mitigate AI-generated citations?
    • Evidence required: Technical papers or industry white papers detailing the efficacy of citation-checking software, cross-referencing databases (e.g., Crossref, DOI verification), and AI-detection algorithms.

Scope

  • Timeframe: January 1, 2023, to the present date (as the 2026 endpoint is in the future, the scope is limited to available data up to the current moment).
  • Domain: Academic and scholarly research, including preprints, peer-reviewed journals, and conference proceedings.
  • Technology: Generative AI models (e.g., ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini) and their application in drafting or referencing academic literature.
  • Geography: Global, with a focus on English-language academic publishing standards.

Exclusions

  • Non-Academic Contexts: AI-generated citations in journalism, marketing, or general web content are excluded unless they directly inform academic practices.
  • Future Predictions: Speculative essays or opinion pieces about what might happen in 2025-2026 that lack empirical grounding or current evidence.
  • General AI Ethics: Broad discussions about AI ethics that do not specifically address the issue of citation integrity or hallucinated references.

Findings

Executive answer

S2 and S3 discuss GenAI automating literature searches and manuscript composition, and the ethical and workflow pressures that follow [S2, S3]; they do not provide evidence of risk at scale. One published framework proposes competency standards for research leadership to benchmark institutional readiness on responsible AI use [S1], but it is a single framework, not evidence of a sector-wide shift. S2 argues the core ethical issue is the unethical use of AI rather than the technology itself [S2], but that is S2's argument, not a literature-wide consensus. Current literature highlights that GenAI is reshaping the traditional resources of editorial and reviewer attention [S3].

1. Prevalence and nature of AI-generated hallucinations

Confidence: Medium (The sources confirm the automation of literature searches but do not provide specific statistical rates of citation hallucinations.)

  • Facts: GenAI is being used to automate parts of the research process, including literature searches [S3]. The use of AI tools in composing academic papers is described as "becoming rampant" [S2].
  • Inference (speculative, not a source claim): If GenAI automates literature searches, fabricated or mismatched citations could follow; S3 does not define this as a structural risk inherent in current workflows [S3].
  • Agent recommendation (not a finding, not a source claim): Researchers should verify all AI-generated literature references against primary databases to ensure accuracy.

2. Institutional and publisher responses

Confidence: High (The sources provide clear evidence of frameworks and editorial pressures.)

  • Facts: A competency framework has been developed for university leadership to benchmark institutional readiness regarding responsible AI use [S1]. Academic journals are facing pressure as GenAI reduces the effort required to create manuscripts, thereby straining editorial attention and reviewer time [S3].
  • Inference: S1's vocabulary could, if adopted, give institutions a shared standard for managing AI integration [S1], but S1 alone does not show a sector-wide movement from reactive to proactive policy.
  • Agent recommendation (not S1's recommendation, not a finding): Universities could consider the competency framework provided by Zyphur (2026) to align research training with responsible AI standards [S1].

3. Ethical and integrity implications

Confidence: High (The sources explicitly discuss the ethical dimensions of AI in scholarship.)

  • Facts: S2 identifies the core ethical issue as the "unethical use" of AI rather than the technology itself [S2]. S3 argues GenAI reshapes the resources editorial and reviewer attention rely on [S3]; it does not claim GenAI threatens the credibility of scientific signals themselves.
  • Inference (hypothetical, not a finding): If automation of research workflows outpaces peer-review verification capacity, the scholarly record could be at risk; S3 discusses the editorial pressure, not this outcome [S3].
  • Agent recommendation (not a finding, not a source claim): Scholarly communities should prioritize the development of ethical guidelines that distinguish between AI-assisted research and AI-generated fabrication.

4. Technical methods for identification and mitigation

Confidence: Low (The provided sources do not detail specific technical software or algorithms for citation verification.)

  • Facts: Current literature on AI in academic writing is described as "scattered" [S4]. While bibliometric analyses are being used to track trends, the provided sources do not list specific technical tools or software for detecting AI-generated citations [S4].
  • Inference: There is a gap between the identification of the problem (unethical use) and the availability of technical mitigation tools in the current literature [S2, S4].
  • Agent recommendation (not a finding, not a source claim): Future research should focus on the efficacy of cross-referencing databases and AI-detection algorithms specifically tailored for citation integrity.

What the evidence does NOT show

  • The evidence does not provide specific statistical data on the frequency or prevalence of AI-hallucinated citations in peer-reviewed journals.
  • The evidence does not identify or evaluate specific technical software (e.g., Crossref, DOI verification tools) currently used to mitigate or detect AI-generated citations.
  • The evidence does not offer empirical data on the success rates of current institutional policies in preventing AI-generated academic misconduct.
