Guide — For journalists

How journalists can verify AI-generated research before publication

Your story goes out under your outlet’s name — including the research an AI assistant gathered for it. Triage the load-bearing claims first, run a four-verdict check at deadline speed, and never publish an unchecked AI-sourced fact.

In short: Your story goes out under your outlet’s name — including the research an AI assistant gathered for it. Triage the load-bearing claims first, run a four-verdict check at deadline speed, and never publish an unchecked AI-sourced fact.

A story is ready to file. Some of the research behind it — background, studies, quotes, numbers — came from an AI assistant: a search tool, a chatbot, a summariser. The copy reads cleanly. The citations are formatted. The question is whether any of it is real — and, if it is not, whose name it goes out under. The answer is yours, and your outlet’s.

Every fact-checking guide you will find speaks to the reader after publication, or to the researcher before writing. None speaks to you, the journalist, between the AI’s output and the publish button — and your job is different from the researcher’s in three ways. First, time: you are checking under a deadline, so you cannot verify every source. Second, stakes: a fabricated study you publish reaches the public under your outlet’s name, and the correction lands on your outlet, not on the tool. Third, remedy: you do not fix the AI — you decide what goes in the story, what gets attributed, and what gets cut.

This guide is built for that job: triage first, a four-verdict check at deadline speed, and a rule for what to do with anything you could not check.

Why this check matters now

The failure mode is not hypothetical, and it is no longer rare. Three documented cases, all verified from primary or reputable secondary reporting, show what reaches a newsroom when nobody checks:

  • AI search tools, March 2025. The Tow Center for Digital Journalism at Columbia tested eight generative search tools — ChatGPT Search, Perplexity, Perplexity Pro, DeepSeek Search, Copilot, Grok-2 and Grok-3, Gemini — by giving each one an excerpt from a news article and asking it to identify the headline, publisher, date and URL. Across 1,600 queries, the tools gave the wrong answer more than 60% of the time. Perplexity, the best performer, was still wrong 37% of the time; Grok 3 was wrong 94% of the time, and 154 of its 200 responses linked to error pages. The tools also tended to cite the wrong article rather than admit they could not find it. These are the same tools journalists now use for research.
  • Public broadcasters, October 2025. The European Broadcasting Union and the BBC, with 22 public broadcasters, tested AI assistants on news questions. In 45% of more than 3,000 answers there was at least one significant issue, and in 31% the attribution was missing, misleading or wrong. The answers were fluent; the sourcing was not.
  • Chicago Sun-Times, May 2025. A syndicated summer reading list carried 15 recommended books. The first 10 did not exist. The list had been assembled with AI help and printed without a check. The paper apologised; the correction ran everywhere the list had run.

None of these is a single journalist’s error. All three are the same failure at different points in the pipeline: material nobody opened before it went out under a trusted name. Your check is the last gate before that failure becomes your story.

The bought-citation problem: a link is not proof either

There is a second failure that a resolution check alone will not catch. A study published 19 November 2026 (Tang et al., “GEOHunter”, arXiv:2611.26564) documents one paid generative-engine-optimisation operation across three major Chinese content platforms — 1,985 accounts, an estimated 466,357 promotional placements. Across nine generative engines, between 4.5% and 20% of retrievable cited pages traced back to this one network, depending on how strictly attribution was counted. On eight of nine engines, the network’s clients were named in 52% of answers.

The practical consequence for a journalist: a citation that resolves to a real page, with a real-looking source, can still be placed there by a paid operation. A link proves a page exists. It does not prove the page is independent evidence. When a cited source looks thin, promotional, or oddly aligned with the claim it supports, ask who benefits from that page being cited — and find a second, independent source before you rely on it.

Oryx’s own published audits show the same shape in AI-generated research. In the larger audit, 194 citations across 30 AI research briefs were checked claim by claim: 50 verified, 95 broken, 17 mismatched, 31 unverifiable, and 1 with no URL at all (Paper 2 v2, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.23731235 — a pilot, not a universal rate). In a smaller study of 66 AI-generated citations, 31 had broken links, 7 mismatched the claim, 11 could not be verified, and 17 both resolved and fully supported the claim (Working Paper v3, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.23718472). Most failures are caught by the first step — asking for the record. The dangerous ones survive it: a real record, correctly cited, that does not support the claim attached to it — or a real page that exists because someone paid for it to be there.

1. Triage first — load-bearing claims before background ones

You cannot check every source in a story under a deadline, and you do not need to. Sort the story’s claims before you open a single source:

  • Load-bearing claims first. The claims the story rests on. If the study under the headline finding fails, the finding fails with it. Check these first, and check them properly.
  • Quotes and numbers second. Every direct quote and every number the story prints. A wrong quote is a named person misquoted under your outlet’s name; a wrong number is a fact your outlet has now published. Both are corrections waiting to happen.
  • Background claims last. Scene-setting context in the opening paragraphs. A failed background citation is a defect worth fixing, but it rarely decides the story.
  • Obscure sources within each tier first. Inside each tier, check the least-known sources before the famous ones. AI-generated research fails most often on obscure material — a study almost nobody has read is where fabrication concentrates, and it is also where a fabricated citation is hardest for anyone else to catch.

A practical split for a typical story: pick the 5–10 sources that carry the argument, and check those thoroughly. Spot-check a further 5–10 across the rest — one or two per section, weighted toward obscure sources. If a spot-check finds a failure, expand the check in that section before filing.

2. The four-verdict check, at newsroom speed

For each source you check, open it yourself — do not take the AI’s word for what it says — and give it one of four verdicts:

  • Verified. The record exists and supports the claim. The title, authors, year and venue match; the passage, table or figure the claim rests on says what the story claims it says.
  • Broken. The record cannot be found. The link is dead, the DOI resolves nowhere or to a different paper, the journal or volume does not exist, or the cited work cannot be located at all.
  • Mismatch. The record exists but does not say what is claimed. The study is real, the passage is real, and the claim is not in it — or the numbers, dates or attributions differ from what the story reports.
  • Unverifiable. The record cannot be checked with the means available: behind a wall you cannot pass, in a language you cannot read, or a source whose existence you cannot confirm either way. Record it as unverifiable, not as verified and not as failed.

The check for one source takes minutes, not hours, if you work in this order: resolve (does the DOI or link open to the cited work?), match (title, authors, year, venue — do they agree?), read (does the passage the claim rests on say what is claimed?), status (is the work what the story says it is — not retracted, not a different edition, not a preprint cited as a published paper?).

Two shortcuts that are not shortcuts: a citation that resolves to a real page is not automatically verified — the mismatch is the failure that survives a resolution check, and it is the one an AI-assembled list hides best. And a source you cannot open is not a pass; it is unverifiable, and it goes to your editor as such.

3. Quotes and numbers — the two things that sink stories

A story can survive a weak background citation. It rarely survives a fabricated quote or a wrong number, because those are the two things readers, subjects and rivals check first.

  • Every direct quote: trace it to the primary source — the speech, the interview transcript, the court filing, the report itself. A quote the AI attributes to a named person must be verifiable in that person’s own words, in a source you have opened. If you can only find the quote repeated in secondary coverage, attribute it as reported, or cut it. Never print a quote whose only source is the AI’s output.
  • Every number: trace it to the primary dataset or report — the study’s own tables, the official statistics release, the company’s own filing. A number the AI gives you without a traceable source is a number you do not have. If the number matters and you cannot trace it, say so in the story (“the figure could not be independently verified”) or leave it out.
  • Every study: check that the study exists, that it says what the story claims, and that it is what the story says it is — peer-reviewed or preprint, published or forthcoming, and not retracted. A preprint cited as a published paper is a mismatch, and it changes what the story can claim.

4. When the AI hands you a study

AI research tools return studies the way a search engine returns pages — with confidence, and sometimes with the wrong paper attached. Three rules cover most cases:

  • Trace to the primary source. If the AI cites a study, open the study. If it cites a news report about a study, open the study behind the report. A claim about research is only as good as the research itself.
  • If only a secondary mention exists, say so. “According to [outlet], the study found…” is honest. Presenting a second-hand account as if you had read the study is not. If the secondary account is the only source and the claim is load-bearing, hold the claim until the primary source is found.
  • Do not accept the AI’s own verification. A note that sources were “verified” by the tool is a claim about the tool, not evidence about these sources. Open them yourself.

5. The deadline workflow

The check scales to the time you have. What does not scale is skipping it for the claims that carry the story.

  • Ten minutes: check the load-bearing claims only. Resolve, match, read. Anything that fails or cannot be checked gets cut or held — it does not go out unverified because the deadline is near.
  • Thirty minutes: add the quotes and the numbers. Every direct quote traced to its primary source; every number traced to its primary dataset or report.
  • An hour or more: add the spot-checks across the rest of the sources, weighted toward obscure ones, and the status check on load-bearing studies (retraction, edition, preprint vs published).
  • If you cannot check it, do not publish it as fact. Attribute it, qualify it, or cut it. “Could not be independently verified” is a sentence your outlet can stand behind. An unchecked AI-sourced fact is a correction your outlet will have to print.

Keep a one-line record as you go: claim, source, verdict, evidence, date. It takes seconds per check, and it is what your editor needs if a reader challenges the story afterwards — and what you need if the story is questioned a month later.

6. How to report a failed source to your editor

A failed source is a finding, not an accusation — against the tool, or against anyone who used it. Your editor needs the evidence, not a judgement about intent.

For each failed or unverifiable source, report four things:

  1. The claim as written — quote the sentence in the draft.
  2. The source as given — the reference exactly as the AI returned it.
  3. What you found — the verdict, and the evidence for it: the dead link, the DOI resolving to a different paper, the passage that says something else, or the wall you could not pass. Give the URL or identifier you checked, and the date.
  4. What it carries — whether the claim is load-bearing, a quote, a number, or background, and whether the story’s conclusion depends on it.

Phrase it as evidence. “The draft cites Chen et al. (2023) for the claim that X. The DOI resolves to a different paper by different authors; a title search returns no paper matching the citation. The claim is load-bearing: it supports the story’s central finding.” That is a finding an editor can act on. “The AI fabricated its sources” is a conclusion about the tool that your check does not support — do not write it.

If you find one failure, say how far you checked: “I checked 8 of 22 sources in full and spot-checked 6 more; 2 failed, 1 is unverifiable.” The editor needs the denominator as much as the failures.

Red flags in AI-gathered research

  • Citations that resolve to a different paper. The DOI or link opens a real paper — by different authors, on a different topic. The identifier-swap is the commonest AI failure in the obscure tier.
  • Perfect-looking citations to studies nobody can find. Full author lists, plausible journals, exact page ranges — and no record anywhere. Fluency is not evidence.
  • Quotes with no primary source. A named person quoted, and the only trace of the quote is the AI’s output or a single secondary repeat.
  • Numbers that move between sources. The figure in the story is not the figure in the source table, or the same number is reported differently in two places the AI cited.
  • A cluster of failures in obscure sources. One failure is a defect; several in the least-known references is a pattern, and it means the research was not checked.
  • Retracted work cited as live. Check retraction status for load-bearing studies — a clean reference can be retracted while the story is in preparation.
  • A source that exists only to be cited. A thin page, a promotional site, a document with no author and no date, aligned exactly with the claim it supports. Ask who benefits from that page being cited, and find a second source.

A note on what this guide does not do

This guide teaches the check. It does not set editorial policy, and it does not tell you whether a story should run — that decision is the editor’s, on the evidence you report and the rest of the reporting. A story that passes this check can still be weak, and a story with a failed background source can still be worth publishing after correction. The check answers one question: do the cited records exist, and do they say what the story claims they say? Everything else in the reporting is still yours.
