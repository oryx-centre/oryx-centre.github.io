How to check an AI vendor's claims before you sign

An AI vendor's demo is a performance. The claims underneath it — accuracy rates, benchmark wins, “grounded in your data” — are what you will actually live with after signing. Here is a six-step check you can run before you sign, using nothing more than the vendor's own materials and a few hours of one reviewer's time.

Step 1: Ask for the claim, in one sentence, with its source

Every material claim should survive being written as a single sentence with a named source attached: “Our model answers 94% of support questions correctly, measured on our 2024 ticket set, version 2.3.” If a claim cannot be written that way — no version, no dataset, no date — it is not a claim yet. It is marketing copy. Send it back and ask for the sentence.

Step 2: Check the citations, not the summary

If the vendor's evidence pack cites papers, benchmarks, or case studies, open a sample yourself. In a published pilot audit of AI-generated briefs, 47% of citations were broken links — the citation looked real and led nowhere (pilot result, 66 citations, Paper 2 (2026)). A vendor pack with dead or mismatched citations has not been checked by its own author. Count what you find: verified, broken, mismatched, unverifiable. A pack that cannot survive its own references cannot support a purchase decision.

Step 3: Ask what version was tested, and what changed since

Model versions move. A benchmark result from three versions ago is history, not evidence about the thing you are buying. Ask for: the exact model and system version behind each number, the date of the measurement, and what changed in the product since. If the vendor cannot tie a number to a version, treat the number as expired.

Step 4: Ask who signs off, and on what record

“Human in the loop” is not an answer. Ask: when the system produces an answer that matters, who reviews it, what evidence do they see, and where is the review recorded? A sign-off with no recorded basis — no evidence shown, no reason written down — is a rubber stamp with better paperwork. You want a named role, a visible evidence trail, and a record you can inspect later.

Step 5: Run your own five-document test

Before any paid pilot, hand the vendor five documents from your own work and five questions you already know the answers to. Ask for sourced answers, then check every citation yourself. This takes an afternoon and tells you more than any benchmark: it tests the product on your material, your questions, and your standard of evidence. If the vendor declines a small test, that decline is itself information.

Step 6: Write the exit into the contract

Ask, before signing: how do you get your data out, what happens to your documents when you leave, and how do you verify the system's answers after the vendor's team stops holding your hand? If the answers are vague, the product is a dependency, not a tool. Put the answers in writing.

Red flags (any one of these should pause a signature)

  • Accuracy percentages with no dataset, version, or date attached.
  • Citations that do not resolve, or resolve to something the claim does not say.
  • “Human oversight” with no named reviewer, no evidence shown, no record kept.
  • Refusal of a small test on your own documents.
  • Benchmarks from a different product version than the one being sold.
  • No written answer on data exit and deletion.

Questions to send the vendor (copy these)

  1. For each accuracy claim: which dataset, which version, measured when?
  2. Can you show me the full citation list behind your evidence pack?
  3. Who reviews high-stakes outputs, what do they see, and where is it recorded?
  4. Will you run a five-document test on our material before we pay?
  5. How do we export our data and verify outputs if we leave?

Worked example (pilot, not a vendor)

In the pilot audit mentioned above, only 5 of 66 citations both resolved and fully supported the claim they were attached to (8%). The point is not that AI tools are useless — it is that a claim without a checked citation is an assertion, and assertions should not be signed for. Run the six steps, keep the record, and let the evidence decide.
