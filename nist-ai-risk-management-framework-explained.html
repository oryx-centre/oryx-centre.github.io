NIST AI Risk Management Framework explained

What the NIST AI Risk Management Framework is, what its four functions — Govern, Map, Measure, Manage — ask you to do, and how to use it on one AI system.

In short: this guide explains what the NIST AI Risk Management Framework is, what its four functions — Govern, Map, Measure, Manage — ask you to do, and how to use it on one AI system. It teaches the method; it is not certification, legal advice, or a compliance determination.

Every organisation using AI now faces the same problem: somebody, sooner or later, will ask how its AI risks are managed — a client, a regulator, a board member, or a vendor’s own customer. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework, usually shortened to the NIST AI RMF, is the most widely used answer to that question. It is not a law, not a checklist, and not a certificate. It is a voluntary method for organising AI risk work, published by the US National Institute of Standards and Technology.

This guide explains the framework the way Oryx explains everything else: as claims that need evidence. Each part of the framework is stated, then translated into the question you would ask and the evidence you would expect to see. A reader who never contacts Oryx should still leave able to read a vendor’s “we follow NIST AI RMF” claim and check whether it points at anything real.

What the NIST AI RMF is

The framework is NIST AI 100-1, Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0), released on 26 January 2023. NIST describes it as voluntary, rights-preserving, non-sector-specific, and use-case agnostic — meaning it is designed to be usable by any organisation, in any sector, for any kind of AI system, and nobody is legally required to follow it. It is free to download from nist.gov.

The framework has two parts. Part 1 explains how to frame AI risk and what NIST means by a trustworthy AI system. Part 2 is the Core: four functions — Govern, Map, Measure, Manage — broken into 19 categories and 72 subcategories that describe outcomes an organisation should be able to show. A companion Playbook suggests actions for reaching those outcomes, and NIST publishes crosswalks that map the framework to other standards. For generative AI specifically, NIST published a companion document, the Generative AI Profile (NIST AI 600-1, 26 July 2024), which names risks that are distinctive to, or made worse by, generative AI — such as confabulation, harmful content, and data leakage.

As of late 2026, NIST has said AI RMF 1.0 is being revised as part of the White House AI Action Plan, with no replacement version published yet. Until a new version is released, version 1.0 is the text to cite. Check the current text on nist.gov before relying on any detail here.

What it is not

Three confusions cause most of the trouble with this framework, so they are worth clearing up first:

  • It is not a law. Nothing in AI RMF 1.0 creates a legal duty. If a law applies to your system — the EU AI Act, for example — the framework can help you organise your response, but following it does not by itself satisfy the law.
  • It is not a certification. No certification scheme exists for the NIST AI RMF, and NIST accredits none. A vendor that shows you a “NIST AI RMF certificate” is showing you its own badge, not a NIST credential. Treat that claim the way you would treat any other unverified claim: ask what was actually done.
  • It is not a checklist you can tick off once. The framework describes outcomes, not a fixed set of controls. Two organisations can both “follow” it and look very different, because their systems, users, and risks differ. That flexibility is the point — and it is also why a claim of following it needs evidence behind it.

What NIST means by a trustworthy AI system

Part 1 of the framework lists the characteristics of a trustworthy AI system. They are worth knowing because they are the standard the four functions are trying to reach:

  • Valid and reliable. The system does what it is supposed to do, consistently, for its intended purpose.
  • Safe. It does not endanger people or property under normal use, or under reasonably foreseeable misuse.
  • Secure and resilient. It resists attack and keeps working — or fails safely — when something goes wrong.
  • Accountable and transparent. Someone is responsible for it, and its behaviour can be explained and examined.
  • Explainable and interpretable. The people affected by its outputs can understand, at the level they need, how those outputs came about.
  • Privacy-enhanced. It protects the personal data it touches.
  • Fair, with harmful bias managed. Its errors and harms are not concentrated on particular groups of people.

No real system is perfect on all seven. The framework’s job is to make the gaps visible, measured, and owned — not to pretend they do not exist.

The four functions: Govern, Map, Measure, Manage

The Core is organised into four functions. Govern runs through everything else; NIST notes that most users then start with Map and move through Measure to Manage, repeating the cycle as systems change.

Govern — who owns AI risk

Govern is the cross-cutting function: the culture, policies, accountability, and oversight that make the rest of the framework possible. In NIST’s words, it is “infused throughout” AI risk management. It has 6 categories and 19 subcategories.

In practice, Govern means: a named person owns AI risk for the organisation; there is a written policy on acceptable AI use; the organisation has agreed, in writing, how much AI risk it will accept; and there is an inventory — one register — of every AI system in use, including systems staff adopted on their own. If nobody owns the risk and nobody knows which AI systems are running, the other three functions have nothing to stand on.

Evidence you would expect to see: the name of the accountable owner, the written policy, the risk-tolerance statement, and the system inventory. If a vendor says it governs its AI, ask for the inventory first — it is the simplest test of whether the claim is real.

Map — what the system is, who it affects, what could go wrong

Map “establishes the context to frame risks related to an AI system”: its purpose, its users, its data, who it affects, and what could go wrong. It has 5 categories and 18 subcategories.

In practice, Map means: for each AI system, write down what it is for, who uses it, which data it touches, who is affected by its outputs, and the ways it could fail or cause harm — including the ways it could fail for particular groups of users. This is also where you record what the system is not for, and where its limits are. A map that lists only the intended uses and never the failure modes is incomplete.

Evidence you would expect to see: a one-page map per system — purpose, users, data, affected parties, failure modes, limits. For a vendor’s system, ask for the map of the system you are buying, not a generic statement about AI risk.

Measure — test the risks you mapped

Measure “employs quantitative, qualitative, or mixed-method tools, techniques, and methodologies” to analyse, assess, benchmark, and monitor AI risk. It has 4 categories and 22 subcategories — the largest of the four functions, because testing is where most of the work sits.

In practice, Measure means: test each mapped risk before launch and keep testing in production. That includes accuracy testing on data like your real data, bias testing across the groups your system affects, security testing, and — for generative AI — testing whether the system invents sources, leaks data, or follows instructions hidden in pasted text. Results are reported as counts and rates, not adjectives: “blocked 17 of 20 direct attempts” is a measurement; “strong safeguards” is not. This is also where the framework asks for the system to be monitored over time, because models, data, and users all drift.

Evidence you would expect to see: the test set, the counts, the date, and the system version the tests ran on. A score without the test set behind it is an unverified claim — ask for the set.

Manage — decide what to do about each risk

Manage “entails allocating risk resources to mapped and measured risks on a regular basis”: prioritise the risks, treat them, respond to incidents, and communicate. It has 4 categories and 13 subcategories.

In practice, Manage means: for each measured risk, someone decides — fix it, accept it in writing, transfer it, or stop the system — and that decision is recorded with a named owner and a date. Incidents get logged and responded to. And when the model, the data, or the guardrails change, the cycle repeats: re-map, re-measure, re-decide. A risk register that has not been updated since launch is a snapshot, not a management system.

Evidence you would expect to see: the risk register with owners, decisions, and dates; an incident log; and a record of the last re-test after the last change.

Profiles: the framework, fitted to one use

The framework also describes profiles — a profile is the framework applied to a specific use case, sector, or system, showing which outcomes matter most there and what the current state is against them. A profile is a useful thing to ask a vendor for: “show me your profile for this system” is a much sharper question than “do you follow NIST?”, because a profile names the specific risks of the specific system and the specific state of each one. The Generative AI Profile (NIST AI 600-1) is itself an example: a profile for generative AI systems, naming 12 risk categories distinctive to generative AI.

How to use it on one system: a seven-step walkthrough

You do not need a governance programme to start. The framework works on a single system, and the steps below are the four functions applied to one case:

  1. Name the owner and the system (Govern). Write down who is accountable for this AI system, what it is for, and how much risk you will accept. Add it to your inventory — even if the inventory is a one-page list.
  2. Map it (Map). One page: purpose, users, data, affected parties, failure modes, limits. Include the ways it could fail for the people least like its designers.
  3. List the tests each risk needs (Measure). For each failure mode, write the test that would catch it — accuracy on real-like data, bias checks across affected groups, security probes, and, for generative AI, a check on whether its citations and claims are real.
  4. Run the tests and record counts (Measure). Report numbers, dates, and versions. Keep the test set fixed and written down so the next run is comparable.
  5. Decide, per risk (Manage). Fix, accept in writing, transfer, or stop — with a named owner and a date for each decision.
  6. Write the profile (Profiles). One page summarising the state of each outcome for this system. This is the document you hand to a client, a board, or an auditor.
  7. Re-run after every change (all four functions). Model update, data change, new user group — re-map, re-measure, re-decide. A framework followed once is a report; a framework repeated is a system.

A worked example (method only, labelled pilot)

Oryx has not published a NIST AI RMF assessment. What it has published is the measurement habit the framework demands, applied to one kind of AI failure. In a pilot audit of 194 AI-generated citations across 30 research briefs (Paper 2, Study 2 — a pilot, not a universal rate), 95 citations (49%) had broken links, 17 (9%) pointed at a real source that did not support the claim, and 31 (16%) could not be verified at all. Only 50 (26%) were verified as real and supporting the claim. Every one of those citations looked plausible. The point for a NIST AI RMF user is the Measure step: a system whose outputs cite sources it invented is failing on validity and reliability, and no amount of Govern paperwork will catch that — only a test that resolves each citation and checks it against the claim will. Map the failure mode, measure it with counts, manage the result. That is the framework in miniature.

Common mistakes

  • Treating the framework as a certificate. There is no NIST AI RMF certificate. A vendor that implies otherwise has misunderstood the framework or is hoping you have.
  • Doing Govern and stopping. A policy document and a risk-tolerance statement are the start, not the system. Without Map and Measure, Govern is a statement of intent with no evidence behind it.
  • Measuring with adjectives. “Robust testing”, “strong safeguards”, “industry-leading” — none of these is a measurement. Ask for the test set, the counts, the date, and the version.
  • Mapping only the intended use. A map that never names a failure mode cannot produce a meaningful test. The failure modes are the point.
  • Treating it as a one-off. The framework is a cycle. A profile written at launch and never updated is out of date the moment the model changes.
  • Confusing it with the law. The framework is voluntary guidance. If a law applies — the EU AI Act, for example — the framework can organise your response, but it does not replace the legal requirements. Oryx’s guide “EU AI Act requirements for high-risk AI systems” explains what that law actually requires.

NIST AI RMF vs the EU AI Act vs ISO/IEC 42001

Readers often meet these three together. They do three different jobs:

  • NIST AI RMF 1.0 is voluntary guidance: a method for organising AI risk work. No law requires it; no certificate exists for it. Use it when you want a flexible, widely recognised way to structure the work.
  • The EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) is a law. For high-risk AI systems it imposes binding requirements — risk management, data governance, human oversight, and more. Use the NIST framework to organise your response to it, not as a substitute for it.
  • ISO/IEC 42001:2023 is a certifiable management-system standard for AI. An organisation can be audited against it and certified. Use it when a client or a contract requires an audited management system, and use the NIST framework’s functions to fill in the risk work inside it.

None of the three is a shortcut for the others. A NIST profile does not prove EU AI Act compliance, and an ISO certificate does not prove a specific system’s outputs are true. Each claim needs its own evidence.

Questions to send a vendor (copy-paste list)

  1. Which parts of the NIST AI RMF have you applied to the system we would buy — and can we see the profile for this system?
  2. Who owns AI risk for this system, and what is your written risk-tolerance statement?
  3. Show us the Map for this system: purpose, users, data, affected parties, failure modes, and limits.
  4. Share the test set behind your Measure results — the tests, the counts, the date, and the system version.
  5. How do you test generative AI risks — confabulation, data leakage, instructions hidden in pasted text?
  6. Show us the risk register: each risk, its owner, the decision taken, and the date.
  7. What happens when the model or the data changes — is there a re-test, and who runs it?
  8. Are incidents logged, and can we see the log for this system?

What to do with the result

  • Pass. The vendor shows a profile for the system, a named owner, a map that includes failure modes, measurements as counts with dates and versions, and a risk register with decisions and owners. Keep the profile with the contract file.
  • Conditional. Named gaps exist — for example, the tests are described but the test set is not shared, or one language or user group is untested. Fix the gaps, re-run the same tests, and record the new counts before signing.
  • Reject. The vendor claims a certificate that does not exist, will not share a profile or a test set, or the measurements show direct failures. A framework claim you cannot see tested is not a framework you can rely on.

Keep the profile — owner, map, measurements, decisions, dates, versions — with the contract file. It is your evidence if the system fails in production, and it is the baseline for the next re-test.
