AI hallucination detection for research

This guide teaches hallucination detection as a practical skill, not a product feature. It covers four hallucination types, a six-step check you can run yourself, and a 20-minute pre-submission routine — all with free tools and a careful reading.

An AI hallucination in research is not a typo. It is a statement that reads as if it were true — a citation that looks complete, a statistic that sounds plausible, a finding attributed to a real paper — and is not. The danger is exactly that polish: a hallucinated citation survives a skim, passes a spellcheck, and only fails when someone actually follows it to its source.

This guide teaches hallucination detection as a practical skill, not a product feature. Everything here can be done with free tools and a careful reading. No software is needed to follow it, and no page of it depends on contacting Oryx.

What a hallucination looks like in a research draft

Hallucinations in research drafts fall into four recurring types. Learning to name them is the first detection skill, because each type fails in a different place.

  1. Fabricated citations. A reference that looks complete — authors, year, journal, volume, pages — but does not exist. No such paper was ever published. This is the most common failure Oryx has measured in its own pilot audits (see the worked example below).
  2. Real paper, wrong claim. The citation exists and resolves, but the paper does not say what the draft claims it says. The paper may say the opposite, say something narrower, or address a different question entirely. This is the hardest type to catch by skimming, because the reference itself checks out.
  3. Invented facts and numbers. A statistic, a date, a sample size, or a quoted result with no source at all, or attributed to a source that contains no such number. Numbers are especially dangerous because they carry an air of precision that discourages checking.
  4. Distorted paraphrase. The source is real and roughly on topic, but the draft’s summary changes its meaning — a correlation becomes a cause, a pilot study becomes a definitive result, a limitation is dropped. The citation resolves, the claim is wrong.

A fifth pattern is worth naming separately: plausible-looking but unverifiable references — a citation to a source you cannot locate anywhere, in any database, under any spelling. It may be a real paper in a language or archive you cannot reach, or it may be invented. Until it is located, treat it as unverified, not as verified.

Why detection is hard — and why it still works

Three things make hallucination detection genuinely difficult:

  • Hallucinations are fluent. A fabricated citation follows every formatting rule. Nothing about its surface marks it as fake.
  • Hallucinations are confident. An AI draft does not hedge its invented facts. The tone of certainty is the same for true and false statements.
  • Hallucinations borrow real details. A fabricated reference often mixes real author names with invented titles, or a real journal with an invented volume. Partial truth makes the whole thing look checked.

Detection works anyway, because a hallucination has one structural weakness: it has no real source behind it. Every detection method in this guide exploits that single fact — follow the claim to its source, and the hallucination has nowhere to hide. The work is methodical rather than clever, and it is the method, not any single tool, that catches the failure.

The 6-step detection check

Run this check on every AI-assisted claim in a draft, not just the ones that look suspicious. Hallucinations do not look suspicious — that is the point.

Step 1 — Separate claims from sources

List every checkable statement in the draft — every factual claim, statistic, quotation, and cited proposition — and write down the source each one is supposed to rest on. A claim with no source attached is not a verified claim; it is an unverified claim, and it goes on the list as such. Slogans (“widely known”, “studies show”, “experts agree”) are not claims until they point at evidence.

Step 2 — Resolve every citation

Follow each DOI or link. Does it resolve to a real, retrievable document? A dead link is a failed citation, not a minor flaw — if the source cannot be reached, the claim it supports cannot be checked, and it must be treated as unverified. Then confirm the document is the one cited: title, authors, year, and journal must match, not just resemble. A real paper with the wrong year or a similar-but-different title is a mismatch, and a mismatch is a failed check.

Free tools for this step: the Crossref DOI resolver (does this DOI exist?), OpenAlex (open bibliographic record), Google Scholar and Semantic Scholar (find the real paper). A perfect-looking reference that appears in none of them is fabricated until proven otherwise.

Step 3 — Read the source against the claim

For each citation that resolves, read the relevant passage and ask: does this source actually say what the draft claims? Mark each item as supports, partially supports, or does not support. A real source saying something else is a failed check, not a pass — this step is where the “real paper, wrong claim” type is caught, and it cannot be skipped or automated away. No resolver or search tool does this step for you; only a reader can.

Step 4 — Trace every number to its origin

Numbers deserve their own pass, because they are the easiest facts to invent and the hardest to notice. For every statistic, percentage, sample size, and date in the draft, find the exact figure in the cited source. If the number is not there, the claim fails — even if the citation itself is real. If the number is there but in a different context (a different year, a different population, a different measure), that is a distortion, and it fails too. Check units and denominators: a rate quoted without its base is not a verified number.

Step 5 — Test the paraphrase

Where the draft summarises a source rather than quoting it, compare the summary against the source’s own words. Watch for the four classic distortions: correlation stated as causation; a pilot or preliminary finding stated as a settled result; a limitation or caveat dropped from the summary; a secondary source cited as if it were the primary one. If the summary changes what the source means, rewrite the summary or drop the claim — do not keep a citation you have shown to be misused.

Step 6 — Record the verdict, per item

Keep a one-line log per item: the claim, the source, the verdict (verified / partially supported / does not support / unverifiable / fabricated), and the date checked. The log is the defence of the draft: if a reviewer asks how you know a claim is sound, the log is the answer. It is also what makes the check repeatable — anyone can re-run it from the log.

Red flags that a draft needs a full check

Any one of these means the whole draft gets the 6-step check, not just the flagged item:

  • Citations that do not resolve, or resolve to the wrong paper.
  • Statistics with no source, or a source that contains no such number.
  • References to papers that cannot be found in Crossref, OpenAlex, Google Scholar, or Semantic Scholar.
  • A claim attributed to a source that says something different on reading.
  • “Studies show” or “research proves” with no named study.
  • A preprint cited as peer-reviewed work, or a secondary citation passed off as primary.
  • Perfect-looking references in a section the author did not write by hand.

Detection tools — what they help with, and what they do not do

  • Crossref DOI resolver: confirms a DOI exists and what it points to. It does not confirm the paper supports your claim.
  • OpenAlex: open bibliographic records — useful for confirming a paper exists and finding its real metadata. It does not read the paper for you.
  • Google Scholar and Semantic Scholar: find the real paper and its citations. They do not verify that the paper says what you claim.
  • AI-detection and “hallucination detector” tools: treat their verdicts with caution. A tool that flags text as “likely AI-generated” says nothing about whether the text is true, and a tool that scores a citation as “likely real” is guessing from patterns, not checking the source. No automated score replaces steps 2–5 above. If a tool’s method is not published and checkable, do not rely on it for a decision that matters.

The honest division of labour: tools help you find sources; you still judge support. Any page or product that promises fully automated hallucination detection is promising something no published method currently delivers.

What to do with a hallucination once you find one

  • Fabricated citation: remove it. Replace the claim with a real source, re-source the claim from a paper you have located, or drop the claim. Never keep a citation you could not verify.
  • Real paper, wrong claim: rewrite the claim to say what the paper actually says, or find a paper that does support the original claim. Do not keep the citation attached to a claim it does not support.
  • Invented number: find the real number in a real source, or remove the number. A statistic without a source is not a statistic.
  • Distorted paraphrase: rewrite the summary faithfully, including the limitation or caveat that was dropped. If the faithful version no longer supports your argument, the argument needs rethinking, not a better paraphrase.
  • Unverifiable reference: keep it marked unverifiable. Do not upgrade it to verified because it looks plausible, and do not cite a paper you have not at least located.

Then re-run the check on the fixed items. A fixed draft is not a verified draft until the fixes themselves have been checked and logged.

A 20-minute pre-submission routine

Run this on the day you submit, after the full 6-step check is done:

  1. Re-run step 2 on every link — links break between drafting and submission.
  2. Re-read the three most important claims against their sources — the abstract’s central claim, the conclusion’s central claim, and the single most surprising finding.
  3. Confirm every number in the abstract and conclusion traces to a verified source in the body.
  4. Confirm no citation in the reference list is one you have never located.
  5. Save the check log with the submitted version, dated.

Worked example (method only, labelled pilot)

The method above is the method Oryx used in its published pilot audits. In a pilot audit of 66 AI-generated citations (Paper 2, Study 1), 31 (47%) had broken links, 7 (11%) mismatched the claim, 11 (17%) could not be verified, and 17 (26%) resolved to a real paper — but only 5 of those 17 fully supported the claim they were cited for. In a larger pilot audit of 194 AI-generated citations across 30 research briefs (Paper 2, Study 2), 95 (49%) had broken links and only 50 (26%) were verified as real and supporting the claim. These are pilot numbers — small, from Oryx’s own audits, not universal rates for all AI-generated research. They are included to show the method working, not to predict how any particular draft will score. The point is the discipline: resolve, then support, then record — for every item, not just the ones that look wrong.

What detection cannot do

Detection has honest limits, and this guide does not hide them:

  • It cannot prove an argument is original, important, or interesting — only that its evidence checks out.
  • It cannot replace peer review. A draft whose citations all check out can still be wrong in its reasoning, its interpretation, or its conclusions.
  • It cannot verify a claim whose source is behind a paywall you cannot access, or in a language you cannot read. Those items are unverifiable until someone with access checks them — record them as such, do not mark them verified.
  • It cannot catch a real source that is itself wrong. Detection checks that the draft faithfully reports its sources; it does not re-do the science in those sources.
  • It cannot guarantee a draft is hallucination-free. A check that finds nothing is evidence the method was run, not proof nothing was missed. That is why the log matters: it shows what was checked, so the next reader knows where to look again.
