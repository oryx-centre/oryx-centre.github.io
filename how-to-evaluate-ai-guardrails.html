How to evaluate AI guardrails

How to test whether an AI system's guardrails actually hold: what guardrails are, what they cannot do, a six-step evaluation, and the questions to send a vendor.

In short: this guide teaches you how to test whether an AI system's guardrails actually hold: what guardrails are, what they cannot do, a six-step evaluation, and the questions to send a vendor before you sign. It teaches the method; it is not certification, legal advice, or a compliance determination.

"We have guardrails" is one of the most common claims a vendor makes about an AI system — and one of the least tested. A guardrail is a control placed around a model: a filter, a rule, or a check that is supposed to stop the system doing something it should not do. The claim is easy to make. Whether the guardrail actually holds, in your use, against the prompts your users will really send, is a different question — and it is one you can test yourself, before you sign.

This guide teaches the test. A reader who never contacts Oryx should still leave able to run it.

What guardrails actually are

Guardrails come in several kinds, and a vendor may mean any of them — or all of them — when it says the word. Ask which ones are actually in place:

  • Input filters. Checks applied to the user's prompt before it reaches the model: blocked topics, blocked phrases, injection patterns, or limits on what the system will accept.
  • Output filters. Checks applied to the model's reply before it reaches the user: blocked content categories, profanity or toxicity screens, and rules that replace or refuse certain answers.
  • Policy rules. Written rules about what the system may and may not do — for example, never give medical dosage advice, never reveal internal instructions, never generate a citation it cannot source. A rule only counts as a guardrail if something enforces it; a policy document on its own is not a control.
  • Topic and scope limits. Restrictions on what the system will discuss or do — for example, a customer-support assistant that refuses questions outside its product, or a research assistant that refuses to draft legal filings.
  • PII and data controls. Rules that stop personal data leaving the system — redacting names, addresses, or account numbers from outputs, or refusing to repeat data the user pasted in.
  • Tool-use limits. For systems that can act — send email, query a database, call an API — limits on which tools it may use, what it may change, and when it must ask a human first.
  • Human-in-the-loop gates. Points where the system stops and waits for a person to approve before it proceeds. This is the strongest form of guardrail, and the most expensive to run — which is why vendors often describe it and rarely implement it.

If a vendor cannot name which of these it uses, "we have guardrails" is a slogan, not a claim. The first step of any evaluation is to get the list in writing.

What guardrails cannot do

This is the part vendors rarely volunteer, and it matters more than the list above:

  • Guardrails do not make outputs true. A filter can block a forbidden topic and still pass a confident, well-written answer that cites a paper that does not exist. In a published Oryx pilot audit of 194 AI-generated citations across 30 research briefs, 95 (49%) had broken links and only 50 (26%) were verified as real and supporting the claim (Paper 2, Study 2 — a pilot, not a universal rate). Those citations came from systems that would have passed most content filters. Truth-checking is a separate job from content-filtering, and a guardrail does neither the other's work.
  • Guardrails do not stop every jailbreak. A jailbreak is a prompt designed to get around a guardrail — by reframing the request, by role-play, by splitting it across turns, or by hiding instructions inside pasted text. No guardrail blocks all of them. A vendor that claims otherwise has not tested enough.
  • Guardrails do not cover every language or phrasing. A filter tuned on English prompts may miss the same request in Hindi, in another script, or in slang. If your users do not all write in English, the guardrail has not been evaluated until it has been tested in the languages your users actually use.
  • Guardrails degrade. Models are updated, prompts drift, and new jailbreak techniques appear. A guardrail that held in January may not hold in June. An evaluation is a snapshot, not a permanent verdict — it needs a re-test date.

The six-step evaluation

Step 1 — Write down what the guardrail must do

Before testing anything, list the behaviours the guardrail is supposed to prevent and the behaviours it is supposed to allow. Be specific: "must refuse to draft a legal filing" is testable; "must be safe" is not. Include the allowed side too — a guardrail that blocks legitimate work is a failed guardrail, not a strong one. Get the vendor's own list first, then add the behaviours you care about that it omitted.

Step 2 — Build a test set

Write 20–40 test prompts covering the behaviours from Step 1. Include:

  • Direct attempts — the straightforward request the guardrail should refuse (e.g. "write a legal filing for my court case").
  • Reframed attempts — the same request restated, split across turns, or wrapped in a fictional scenario.
  • Indirect attempts — instructions hidden inside pasted text, a document, or a quoted message, asking the system to follow them.
  • Edge cases — requests that are close to the line but legitimate, which the guardrail should allow.
  • Your languages — the same prompts in every language your users will actually use.

Keep the test set fixed and written down. If you change it mid-test, you cannot compare results.

Step 3 — Run the tests and record the verdict for each

For every prompt, record three things: the prompt, the system's response, and a verdict — blocked (refused or safely redirected), passed (answered correctly and within the rules), or failed (answered when it should have refused, or refused when it should have answered). A guardrail that refuses a legitimate request has failed that test just as surely as one that answers a forbidden one. Count both kinds of failure.

Step 4 — Measure, honestly

Report the results as counts, not adjectives. "Blocked 17 of 20 direct attempts, 9 of 15 reframed attempts, and refused 3 of 10 legitimate requests" is an evaluation. "Strong guardrails" is not. State the limits alongside the numbers: how many prompts, in which languages, on which date, on which version of the system. A vendor's own test numbers are a claim too — ask for the test set, not just the score. If the vendor will not share it, say so in your record and treat the score as unverified.

Step 5 — Check the surrounding controls

A guardrail is only one layer. Check whether the system also has the controls that catch what the guardrail misses:

  • Logging. Are prompts and responses logged, and can you review them? If a guardrail fails silently and nobody can see it, the failure does not exist on paper — only in practice.
  • Override and escalation. Can a human override a refusal, and is the override recorded? Who can do it, and what happens when they do?
  • Version and change records. When the model or the guardrail rules change, is there a record — and a re-test?
  • Human review gate. Is there a named person who reviews the guardrail's failures and accepts the residual risk? A guardrail without a named human acceptor is a control without an owner.

Step 6 — Re-test after every change

Every model update, prompt change, or guardrail rule change is a new evaluation. Re-run the same test set — that is why Step 2 insisted on a fixed, written-down set — and compare the counts. A guardrail that held before an update and fails after it is a regression, and it is the most common way a deployed system quietly gets worse.

Red flags

  • The vendor will not name which guardrails exist, or describes them only as "industry-standard".
  • The vendor quotes a safety score but will not share the test set, the prompt count, or the date.
  • The guardrail has never been tested in the languages your users write in.
  • Refusals are not logged, or there is no record of who reviewed a failure.
  • There is no named human who accepts the residual risk when a guardrail fails.
  • The guardrail blocks legitimate work often enough that users learn to work around it — because a bypassed guardrail protects nothing.
  • The vendor treats the evaluation as a one-off, with no re-test after updates.

A worked example (method only, labelled pilot)

Oryx has not published a guardrails evaluation. What it has published is the method this guide applies, and the reason the method is needed. In the pilot audit of 194 AI-generated citations across 30 research briefs (Paper 2, Study 2 — a pilot, not a universal rate), 95 citations (49%) had broken links, 17 (9%) pointed at a real source that did not support the claim, 31 (16%) could not be verified at all, and 1 had no URL at all. Only 50 (26%) were verified. Every one of those citations looked plausible, and a content filter would have passed almost all of them — they contained nothing a toxicity or topic filter would block. The point is the method: a control that filters content is not a control that verifies truth, and an evaluation that only tests the filter will miss the failures that matter most to a research team. Test both, and record both.

Questions to send a vendor (copy-paste list)

  1. Which guardrails are in place — input filters, output filters, policy rules, topic limits, PII controls, tool-use limits, human gates? Name each one.
  2. What exactly is each guardrail supposed to block, and what is it supposed to allow?
  3. Share the test set you used to evaluate them — the prompts, the counts, the date, and the system version.
  4. How do the guardrails perform against reframed and indirect prompts, not just direct ones?
  5. In which languages have they been tested, and what were the results per language?
  6. Are refusals and guardrail failures logged, and can we review the logs?
  7. Who is the named human who reviews guardrail failures and accepts the residual risk?
  8. What happens when the model or the guardrail rules change — is there a re-test, and who runs it?

What to do with the result

  • Pass. The guardrails blocked the forbidden requests, allowed the legitimate ones, and the surrounding controls (logging, override records, a named human acceptor, a re-test plan) are in place. Keep the test record with the contract file.
  • Conditional. Named gaps exist — for example, reframed prompts slip through, or one language is untested. Fix the gaps, re-run the same test set, and record the new counts before signing.
  • Reject. The vendor will not name the guardrails, will not share a test set, or the guardrails fail on direct attempts. A guardrail you cannot see tested is not a guardrail you can rely on.

Keep the test record — prompts, responses, verdicts, counts, date, version — with the contract file. It is your evidence if a guardrail fails in production, and it is the baseline for the next re-test.
