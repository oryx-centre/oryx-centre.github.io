AI safety audit checklist: check before you deploy

A practical AI safety audit checklist: test what the system actually does, check its guardrails and human oversight, and record what you found — before it meets real users.

In short: run this seven-section checklist on any AI system before it meets real users: test what it actually does, check the guardrails that are supposed to catch failures, check the human oversight, and record what you found. This guide teaches safety auditing; it is not certification, and it does not promise a system is safe.

An AI system that works well in a demo can still fail badly in use. It can invent facts, follow instructions it was never meant to follow, leak data it was supposed to keep private, or quietly behave differently on real inputs than it did on the test set. A safety audit is the check you run before that system meets real users: you test what it actually does, you check the controls that are supposed to catch failures, and you record what you found so someone can be held to it.

This guide is a checklist you can run on any AI system you are about to deploy or buy — a chatbot, a document-drafting tool, a research assistant, a customer-facing agent. You need no product and no paid tool to follow it. What you need is access to the system itself, a set of test inputs, and someone with the authority to say no.

What a safety audit is — and what it is not

A safety audit answers one question: does the evidence support the claim that this system is safe enough to use for this purpose? It is not a one-off test, not a vendor's marketing slide, and not a certificate. Three distinctions matter:

  • Testing is not auditing. Running a few prompts and liking the answers is testing. Auditing means deciding in advance what you will test, recording each result, and keeping the record.
  • A claim is not evidence. "Our model is safe", "enterprise-grade guardrails", "fully tested" are slogans until they point at something checkable: a test, a log, a named person who signed off. The audit's job is to separate the claim from the evidence for it.
  • Safe for what? A system can be safe enough for drafting internal summaries and unsafe for giving medical or legal advice to the public. Every check below is tied to the system's actual use, not to safety in the abstract.

The method is the same one Oryx uses for citation audits: separate each claim from its source, verify the source, and record who accepted the residual risk. Applied to safety, the "claims" are the system's safety promises and the "sources" are its tests, logs, and controls.

Why AI systems fail in ways a checklist can catch

In a published pilot audit of 66 AI-generated citations (Paper 2, study 1 — a pilot, not a universal rate), 31 links (47%) were broken, 7 citations (11%) mismatched the claim they were attached to, 11 (17%) could not be verified at all, and only 5 both resolved and fully supported the claim. In a second pilot of 30 AI-generated research briefs (study 2 — a pilot), 194 citations were checked: 50 verified (26%), 95 broken (49%), 17 pointed to a real source that did not say what was claimed (9%), and 31 could not be verified (16%).

Those numbers are about citations, not about safety. They are quoted here for one reason: they show the failure pattern of AI output in general. An AI system does not fail by announcing that it is wrong. It fails by producing plausible output that is wrong in ways you only find by checking — a real-looking reference that points nowhere, a real source cited for a claim it does not make. A safety audit assumes the same pattern will show up in behaviour, not just in citations: the system will look safe until you test the input it was not designed for.

The checklist

Work through the seven sections in order. For each item, mark pass, fail, or not checked — never leave an item blank, and never mark "pass" on the basis of a vendor's word alone. The record is the audit.

1. Know what you are auditing

  • ☐ The system's purpose is written down in one sentence: what it does, for whom, in which setting.
  • ☐ The inputs it will receive and the outputs it will produce are described — including the inputs it is not supposed to receive.
  • ☐ The people affected by its outputs are named: users, customers, staff, or the public.
  • ☐ The harms that matter most for this use are listed before testing starts (wrong facts, harmful advice, leaked data, biased decisions, service failure). A harm you did not name is a harm you will not test for.

2. Test what it actually does

  • ☐ A fixed set of test inputs exists, covering normal use, edge cases, and hostile inputs — including prompts that try to get the system to ignore its instructions.
  • ☐ Each test input has an expected result written down before the test is run, not decided afterwards.
  • ☐ The system was tested on inputs it had not seen during development, not only on the examples it was tuned against.
  • ☐ Failures are counted and recorded, not explained away: how many test inputs produced a wrong, unsafe, or off-instructions output, out of how many tested.
  • ☐ The test was run on the version being deployed, not an earlier or later one — and the version number is recorded.

3. Check the guardrails

Guardrails are the controls that sit between the system and the user: filters, refusal rules, output checks, and limits on what the system may do. A guardrail that has never been tested is a claim, not a control.

  • ☐ Each guardrail is named, with what it is supposed to block or catch.
  • ☐ Each guardrail was tested with inputs designed to get past it — and the test results are recorded.
  • ☐ The system refuses clearly when it should refuse, instead of answering with a plausible-sounding substitute.
  • ☐ There is a record of what happens when a guardrail fails: who is alerted, and what stops the output reaching the user.
  • ☐ No guardrail depends on the user noticing the problem. If the only safety net is "the user will spot it", there is no safety net.

4. Check the data and the evidence behind it

  • ☐ The data the system was built or tuned on is described — where it came from, how old it is, and what it does not cover.
  • ☐ Any factual claim the system makes can be traced to a source that resolves and actually supports it. (For the check itself, see Oryx's guide "How to check AI-generated citations in your own paper".)
  • ☐ Out-of-date data is flagged: the system does not present old information as current.
  • ☐ Personal or confidential data in the training or retrieval data is identified, and there is a stated rule for what the system may do with it.

5. Check the human oversight

No AI system should make a consequential decision with no human in the loop. "Human oversight" means a named person with the time, the information, and the authority to intervene — not a disclaimer.

  • ☐ A named person is responsible for the system's outputs in this deployment, and that person knows they are responsible.
  • ☐ The human reviewer sees enough to judge: the output, the sources it relied on, and the confidence or uncertainty behind it — not just the final answer.
  • ☐ There is a defined point where the human can stop, correct, or override the system before the output reaches anyone affected.
  • ☐ The reviewer has time to review. A queue of a thousand outputs a day with one reviewer is not oversight; it is a rubber stamp.
  • ☐ High-stakes outputs (anything affecting money, health, legal rights, or safety) always go through a human before they are acted on.

6. Check failure handling and incident response

  • ☐ There is a written procedure for what happens when the system produces a harmful or wrong output — who is told, how fast, and what is switched off.
  • ☐ Outputs and key decisions are logged in a way that lets you reconstruct what happened after an incident.
  • ☐ There is a way for users to report a bad output, and reports are reviewed by a person.
  • ☐ The system can be paused or rolled back to a previous version without a rebuild.

7. Check the documentation and the record

  • ☐ The system's purpose, limits, test results, and known failure modes are written down in one place a new team member could read.
  • ☐ The limits are stated honestly: what the system is not for, and what it has not been tested on.
  • ☐ The audit record itself is kept with the deployment file: date, version tested, test inputs, results, and the name of the person who signed off.
  • ☐ There is a date for the next re-audit — after a model update, a data update, or a change in use, whichever comes first. An audit of last year's system is not an audit of this year's system.

Red flags

Stop and escalate if you find any of these. Each one on its own is a reason not to deploy until it is fixed:

  • No test record. The vendor or team cannot show what was tested, on which version, with what result. "We tested it thoroughly" without a record is a slogan, not evidence.
  • Guardrails that cannot be demonstrated. Nobody can show you a guardrail failing safely, because nobody has tried to break it.
  • No named human owner. Responsibility is spread across a team, a vendor, and a disclaimer — which means nobody owns a failure.
  • The system cannot be paused. Once deployed, there is no way to stop it quickly if it misbehaves.
  • Safety claims that cite no evidence. A claim about safety that points at no test, no log, and no person is exactly the kind of claim the audit exists to catch.

A worked example: auditing one safety claim

Take a single claim a vendor makes — for instance, "our research assistant only cites real sources." Treat it the way a citation audit treats a citation: resolve it, then check support, then record.

  1. Ask for the evidence in writing. Which test, on which version, measured citation accuracy? How many citations were checked, and how many failed?
  2. Check the evidence resolves. Is there a test report you can read, or only a slide? A claim whose evidence cannot be produced is unverified, not verified.
  3. Check the evidence supports the claim. If the test found that 26% of citations verified and 49% were broken (the pilot pattern in Paper 2, study 2), the claim "only cites real sources" does not survive — whatever the marketing says.
  4. Record the verdict. Claim, evidence requested, evidence received, verdict (supports / partially supports / does not support), date, and who signed off. Keep it with the contract file.

One claim checked properly teaches more than a hundred claims accepted on trust.

What to do with the result

  • Pass: every item marked pass, with evidence, on the version being deployed. Deploy, and set the re-audit date.
  • Conditional: named gaps with named fixes and a re-check date. Deploy only the parts that passed, if the failed items can be isolated — otherwise wait.
  • Reject: any red flag unresolved, or a failure rate in testing that no fix has addressed. Do not deploy on a promise to fix it later; the audit's verdict is about the system as it stands today.

Whatever the verdict, keep the checklist and the test record. If the system later causes harm, the record shows what was checked, what was found, and who accepted the risk — and if it shows a gap nobody checked, that is the first thing to fix next time.

How this relates to recognised frameworks

Two public frameworks cover much of the ground above, and they are useful as cross-checks, not as substitutes for testing your own system:

  • NIST AI Risk Management Framework 1.0 organises risk management into four functions — Govern, Map, Measure, Manage. This checklist's sections map to them loosely: sections 1 and 7 are Govern and Map; sections 2–4 are Measure; sections 5 and 6 are Manage. If you already use the NIST framework, use this checklist as the evidence-gathering step inside it.
  • The EU AI Act sets obligations for high-risk AI systems, including risk management, data governance, human oversight, and record-keeping. This guide is not legal advice and does not tell you whether a system is high-risk or what the Act requires of you — for that, read the Act itself or consult a qualified adviser. What the checklist does is give you the evidence habits the Act's obligations assume: test, record, name the human, keep the file.

A framework tells you what good looks like. An audit tells you whether you have it. Do not confuse the two.

What this checklist cannot do

Checking a system's safety claims verifies the evidence for those claims on the day of the check. It does not prove the system will be safe tomorrow, after an update, or on inputs nobody tested. It does not verify the system's underlying quality — a system can pass every safety check and still be useless at its job. And it does not replace the judgments that belong to the people deploying it: whether the residual risk is worth taking, and who bears it if the system fails. Those are decisions, and they need a name attached.
