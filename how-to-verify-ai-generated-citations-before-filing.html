Guide — For lawyers

How lawyers can verify AI-generated citations before filing

A filing goes out under your name and your bar number — including the cases an AI assistant found for it. Triage the load-bearing authorities first, run a four-verdict check at filing speed, and never file an unchecked AI-sourced citation.

In short: Triage the load-bearing authorities first, run a four-verdict check at filing speed, and never file an unchecked AI-sourced citation. This guide teaches citation checking; it is not legal advice.

A brief is ready to file. Some of the authorities in it — cases, statutes, holdings — came from an AI assistant: a chatbot, a research tool, a summariser. The citations are formatted. The pin cites look precise. The question is whether any of it is real — and, if it is not, whose name it goes out under. The answer is yours, and your bar number's.

Every citation guide you will find speaks to the researcher after the draft is written, or to the reader after publication. None speaks to you, the lawyer, between the AI's output and the filing — and your job is different from the researcher's in three ways. First, time: you are checking under a filing deadline, so you cannot verify every authority. Second, stakes: a fabricated case you file reaches a court under your name, and the sanction lands on you, not on the tool. Third, remedy: you do not fix the AI — you decide what goes in the brief, what gets verified, and what gets cut.

This guide is built for that job: triage first, a four-verdict check at filing speed, and a rule for what to do with anything you could not check.

Why this check matters now

The failure mode is not hypothetical, and it now carries sanctions. Two documented cases, both verified from primary or reputable secondary reporting, show what reaches a court when nobody checks:

  • Ohio, 24 November 2026. The Ohio Supreme Court indefinitely suspended attorney Arleesha Wilson. The brief at issue, filed in a federal case in April 2025, cited four AI-hallucinated cases that did not exist — and the filing was followed by false statements to the courts and other ethics violations. The discipline record also includes more than $647,000 in judgments for neglecting client matters and a $7,500 contempt penalty. The suspension was not for using AI. It was for filing what the AI produced without checking it, and then for what followed.
  • New York, April 2026. An emergency motion filed in a bankruptcy court contained dozens of errors including AI "hallucinations" — fabricated case citations, misquoted holdings, non-existent statutes — caught by opposing counsel. Sullivan & Cromwell apologised to the court in writing, said its AI policies were not followed, and filed a corrected version. The firm survived the episode. The brief did not.

Neither case is a single lawyer's error. Both are the same failure at different points in the pipeline: material nobody opened before it went out under a trusted name. Your check is the last gate before that failure becomes your filing.

The prevalence gradient: check obscure authorities hardest

Fabrication does not spread evenly. A study published 17 November 2026 (Zhong et al., "Dreaming Up Authors", arXiv:2611.23530; EMNLP 2026) tested three frontier language models on academic authorship attribution across 1.92 million papers in eight disciplines, using citation count as a proxy for how prevalent a paper is in training data. The result is a steep gradient: hallucination rates exceed 98% for rarely cited papers and fall below 37% for highly cited ones, holding across all models and disciplines.

The practical rule follows directly: the more obscure the cited authority, the harder you check it. A citation to a famous case is usually right, because the model has seen it many times. A citation to a case almost nobody has read is where fabrication concentrates — and it is also where a fabricated citation is hardest for anyone else to catch, because almost nobody has read the source either. In a brief, that means the string cite of minor authorities and the one-off case that carries a sub-argument deserve more of your time than the leading case everyone knows.

Oryx's own published audits show the same shape in AI-generated research. In the larger audit, 194 citations across 30 AI research briefs were checked claim by claim: 50 verified, 95 broken, 17 mismatched, 31 unverifiable, and 1 with no URL at all (Paper 2 v2, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.23731235 — a pilot, not a universal rate). In a smaller study of 66 AI-generated citations, 31 had broken links, 7 mismatched the claim, 11 could not be verified, and 17 both resolved and fully supported the claim (Working Paper v3, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.23718472). Most failures are caught by the first step — asking for the record. The dangerous ones survive it: a real record, correctly cited, that does not support the claim attached to it.

1. Triage first — load-bearing authorities before background ones

You cannot check every authority in a brief under a filing deadline, and you do not need to. Sort the brief's citations before you open a single source:

  • Load-bearing authorities first. The cases and statutes the argument rests on. If the case under the central proposition fails, the proposition fails with it. Check these first, and check them properly.
  • Quotes and holdings second. Every direct quote from a judgment and every holding the brief attributes. A wrong quote is a court misquoted under your name; a wrong holding is a proposition your brief has now asserted to a judge. Both are sanctions waiting to happen.
  • Background authorities last. Scene-setting citations in the introduction and statement of facts. A failed background citation is a defect worth fixing, but it rarely decides the brief.
  • Obscure authorities within each tier first. Inside each tier, check the least-known authorities before the famous ones. A citation to a case with a handful of citations is where fabrication concentrates.

A practical split for a typical brief: pick the 5–10 authorities that carry the argument, and check those thoroughly. Spot-check a further 5–10 across the rest — one or two per section, weighted toward obscure authorities. If a spot-check finds a failure, expand the check in that section before filing.

2. The four-verdict check, at filing speed

For each authority you check, open it yourself — do not take the AI's word for what it says — and give it one of four verdicts:

  • Verified. The record exists and supports the claim. The caption, citation, court and year match; the passage, paragraph or holding the claim rests on says what the brief claims it says.
  • Broken. The record cannot be found. The citation resolves nowhere or to a different case, the reporter or volume does not exist, or the cited authority cannot be located at all.
  • Mismatch. The record exists but does not say what is claimed. The case is real, the passage is real, and the holding is not in it — or the facts, dates or disposition differ from what the brief reports.
  • Unverifiable. The record cannot be checked with the means available: behind a wall you cannot pass, in a jurisdiction whose reporter you cannot reach, or an authority whose existence you cannot confirm either way. Record it as unverifiable, not as verified and not as failed.

The check for one authority takes minutes, not hours, if you work in this order: resolve (does the citation open to the cited case?), match (caption, court, year, reporter — do they agree?), read (does the passage the claim rests on say what is claimed?), status (is the authority what the brief says it is — not overruled, not a different court, not a slip opinion cited as a reported decision?).

3. What to do with each verdict

  • Verified: file it. Keep the note of what you checked — the passage and the date — so the check is retraceable if anyone asks.
  • Broken: cut it. Do not replace it with another AI-suggested authority. Find the replacement yourself, from a source you have opened, or drop the proposition it supported.
  • Mismatch: cut the claim, not just the citation. A real case cited for a proposition it does not support is worse than a missing citation: it borrows authority the proposition has not earned. Rewrite the proposition to what the case actually holds, or drop it.
  • Unverifiable: do not file it as if it were verified. Either verify it through another route before filing, or cut it. "I could not check it" is a reason to remove the citation, never a reason to file it anyway.

4. The filing-day checklist

Before the brief goes out, run this once, on paper, for every authority in the load-bearing tier:

  1. I opened the source myself. (Not the AI's summary of it.)
  2. The caption, court, year and reporter match the citation.
  3. The passage I rely on says what the brief says it says.
  4. The authority is good law for the proposition — not overruled, not distinguishable on the facts the brief omits.
  5. Anything I could not check is out of the brief.

If any item fails, the brief is not ready. The check is the work; the filing is the receipt.

The standard, in one sentence

A citation counts only when a reader can retrace it to its source and see whether the source actually supports the claim — and in a filing, that reader is a judge, and the claim is yours.
