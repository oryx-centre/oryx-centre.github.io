Research AI: what it is, what it gets wrong, and how to check it

Research AI can read a hundred papers in minutes and draft the summary for you. It can also cite papers that do not exist and attach real sources to claims they never supported. This guide explains what research AI is, what it gets wrong, and how to check its output before you trust it.

In short: research AI is software that searches, summarises and drafts from research literature. In our audit of 194 citations in 30 AI-generated research briefs, only 50 were verified — 95 were broken, 17 were title mismatches, 31 were unverifiable, and 1 had no link at all. Check every citation before you use the output: open the source, match the claim to what the source actually says, and label what you cannot verify.

What research AI is

Research AI is software that finds, summarises, analyses and drafts from research literature. You give it a question; it returns papers, summaries or a drafted report, with citations attached. It comes in three broad categories:

  • Literature-discovery tools. Search across the published record and return papers matching a question, often with a short summary of each one.
  • Deep-research agents. Take a question, browse many sources on their own, and return a synthesised report with citations.
  • Writing assistants. Draft text from sources you provide, or sources the tool finds, producing paragraphs, summaries and reference lists.

What the three share is the output: text that reads as researched, with citations attached. The citations are the part to check. A fluent summary with a broken citation is not research — it is a draft that still needs a reader.

What research AI gets wrong

We measured this ourselves, on AI-generated research. Our 194-citation audit covered 30 AI-generated research briefs (DOI 10.5281/zenodo.23731235): 50 citations verified (26%), 95 broken (49%), 17 title mismatches (9%), 31 unverifiable (16%), and 1 with no link at all. A second, smaller study of 66 citations (working paper v3.0, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.23718472) found 31 broken, 17 verified, 7 mismatched and 11 unverifiable — only 5 claims were both resolved and fully supported. Three failure modes explain most of the damage:

  • Citations that do not exist. A complete-looking reference — authors, journal, year, a plausible title — that resolves to nothing. Nearly half the citations in the 194-citation audit were broken. This is the most common failure, and the easiest to catch: click the link.
  • Real sources attached to the wrong claim. The source exists, but it does not say what the sentence claims. Seventeen of 194 were title mismatches. This is the most dangerous failure, because it looks checked: the link works, the paper is real, and the claim is still unsupported.
  • Confident summaries that smooth over gaps. The draft reads smoothly and hides what it could not find, instead of labelling the gap. Thirty-one of 194 citations could not be verified at all. A gap that is labelled is a gap you can fix; a gap that is smoothed over is an error waiting for a reader to find.

The pattern behind all three is the same: the output is fluent and confident, so the errors hide inside sentences that read well. A wrong citation in a paper risks a reviewer’s correction. In a briefing, a filing or a decision note, it risks more. The fluency is not evidence — the source is.

How to check research-AI output

Use the same method we use in our audits. Give every citation one verdict — Verified, Broken, Mismatch, or Unverifiable — and work through the output in five steps:

  1. Open the cited source. Click every link, not a sample. A link that does not resolve is a failed citation, full stop.
  2. Match the exact claim to what the source actually says. Read the abstract, not just the title. If the source is on a related topic but does not support your sentence, that is a mismatch — rewrite the sentence to match the source, or find a source that matches the sentence.
  3. Check the citation exists at all. Cross-check the title, authors and year against a scholarly index or the publisher’s own page. Year, volume, page numbers and author lists are the details that get invented most often; one wrong detail means the rest needs checking too.
  4. Label gaps rather than smoothing them over. If you cannot reach a source or cannot confirm what it says, say so in the draft. An unverifiable claim is not a verified one, and a labelled gap is honest where a smoothed-over gap is not.
  5. Keep a one-line record per citation. Citation, verdict, and the source you actually opened. It takes about a minute per citation, and it saves you twice: first when someone asks what you checked, and second when you revisit the work months later and cannot remember.

Anything that is not Verified should be fixed, replaced, or cut — including the sentence it supports.
