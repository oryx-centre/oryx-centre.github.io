How to check AI-generated statistics before you cite them

AI drafts statistics that look precise and sourced. Before you cite a number, trace it to its source table, check the denominator, and recompute it.

In short: A wrong statistic does not look wrong — it has a decimal point, a percentage sign, and a real source name attached to it. Before you cite a number, trace it to its source table, check the denominator, and recompute it. This guide teaches statistics checking; it is not a guide to doing statistics.

A wrong citation is easy to spot once you open the link. A wrong statistic is harder, because it does not look wrong. It has a decimal point, a percentage sign, and a source name attached to it. "47% of email recipients open based on the subject line (Mailchimp)" reads like a fact. In a test of 20 AI-written blog posts, that exact claim was one of the ones that could not be traced to any source at all — the number was precise, the attribution was a real company, and the figure itself had no home.

That is the failure this guide exists to catch. A citation check asks whether a reference exists and supports a claim. A statistics check asks a different question: does this number come from the place it claims to come from, measured the way it claims to have been measured? A number can pass the citation check and still be wrong — the source is real, the link works, and the figure in the source is not the figure in your draft. The check below is how to check AI-generated statistics before you cite them: trace each figure to its source table, check the denominator, and recompute it.

Why a statistic fails differently

Three properties make AI-generated numbers harder to check than AI-generated citations:

  • Precision feels like proof. A vague claim invites doubt. A number with one decimal place invites trust. Nothing about "91.5%" makes it more likely to be true than "about nine in ten" — the decimal is formatting, not evidence.
  • The attribution is often real. The failure is rarely a made-up source. In the Skryvo test described below, 54 of the 113 unconfirmed or contradicted claims arrived with a named attribution — a real company, a real report, a real-sounding study. The number was attached to a source that never said it.
  • A citation check does not catch it. Open the source, find the paper exists, and the citation passes. But the number in your draft may come from a different table, a different year, a different population, or a different denominator inside that same real source. The citation is verified. The statistic is not.

Oryx's own published audits show the shape of the problem in research writing. In the larger audit, 194 citations across 30 AI research briefs were checked claim by claim: 50 verified, 95 broken, 17 mismatched, 31 unverifiable, and 1 with no URL at all (Paper 2 v2, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.23731235 — a pilot, not a universal rate). The mismatch verdict — 17 citations — is the one that matters here: a real source, correctly cited, that does not support the claim attached to it. A misused number is the same failure with digits instead of words. In a smaller study of 66 AI-generated citations, 31 had broken links, 7 mismatched the claim, 11 could not be verified, and 17 both resolved and fully supported the claim (Working Paper v3, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.23718472).

The five failure modes

Every bad AI-generated statistic falls into one of five shapes. Name the shape before you decide what to do about it:

  1. The invented number. No source anywhere says it. The figure was generated to fit the sentence. This is the most common failure in the Skryvo test: of 186 checkable claims in 20 AI-written blog posts, 105 (56.5%) could not be traced to any source, and 110 of the 113 unconfirmed or contradicted claims contained a number. The model rarely invents opinions; it invents figures.
  2. The real number, wrong denominator. The digits exist in the source, but the base is different from the one your draft implies. "47% of users" becomes "47% of all adults" when the source measured only paying subscribers. The number is real. The claim is false.
  3. The stale number. The figure was true once and is quoted as if it were true now. A 2019 survey result presented as the current state of a market, with no date in the sentence to warn the reader.
  4. The transposed or rounded number. Digits swapped, a decimal moved, a range collapsed into a point estimate. "1.7%" becomes "7.1%"; "between 12% and 18%" becomes "15%". Small errors, large consequences when the number carries an argument.
  5. The number from a different population. The figure is real and current, but it describes a subgroup — one country, one age band, one income bracket — and your draft presents it as the whole. A figure lifted from a subgroup and presented as the whole-population result is a mis-extraction even when the digits are right.

A preprint study of AI research agents found the same pattern from the other direction: researchers manually labelled 256 numerical claims from 18 agent-written submissions and found that about four in five unsupported numerical claims were values present in the agent's own files but lacking support from the appropriate source — misattribution, not invention, was the major failure mode (Alfaqeeha et al., preprint, 2026). The number is usually somewhere in the material. It is usually attached to the wrong claim.

The number's paper trail

Before you check a statistic, know what a checked statistic looks like. Every number you cite should be traceable to five facts:

  • The source table or dataset. Not the report, the table. A report can contain a hundred tables; "the World Bank says" is not a source, it is a gesture. The table number, the dataset name, or the page is the source.
  • The denominator. What the percentage is a percentage of. A rate without a stated base is not a statistic, it is a slogan with a percent sign.
  • The reference period. When the number was measured. A figure without a date is a figure you cannot place in time, and time is usually what makes it wrong.
  • The population. Who was measured — which country, which age band, which group. A number about one population quoted as a number about another is failure mode 5.
  • The units. Percent, percentage points, per thousand, currency, constant or current prices. "Grew by 5%" and "grew by 5 percentage points" are different claims, and AI drafts confuse them.

If you cannot fill in all five for a number in your draft, the number is not checked yet. It may still be right — but you do not know that, and neither does your reader.

The 6-step check

Run this on every AI-assisted statistic before it goes out under your name. Check the load-bearing numbers first — the ones in the executive summary, the ones the recommendation rests on, the ones a reader will quote — then work through the rest.

  1. Isolate the number and its claim. Write down the figure and, separately, what the sentence claims it means. "Unemployment fell to 4.1% in Q3" is two things: a figure (4.1%) and a claim (it fell, in Q3, for the population the sentence implies). Check both.
  2. Find the source table, not just the source. Open the cited report or dataset and find the exact table the number should come from. If the citation names only the organisation, or only the report, you have not found the source yet — you have found the building it lives in.
  3. Check the denominator first. Before comparing digits, confirm the base. What is the percentage of? If the source table's denominator is not the one your sentence implies, stop here: the number is failure mode 2, whatever the digits say.
  4. Check the date, population and units. Does the source table cover the same period, the same group, and the same units as your sentence? A figure from the right table but the wrong year is failure mode 3. A figure from the right table but a subgroup is failure mode 5.
  5. Recompute where the data allows. If the source gives the numerator and the denominator, do the division yourself. If it gives a table, add the column. Recomputing takes a minute and catches transpositions (failure mode 4) that no amount of reading will catch, because the wrong digits look exactly like the right ones.
  6. Log the verdict. Verified, invented, wrong denominator, stale, transposed, or wrong population — one of the six, no "probably fine". A number whose verdict is not recorded has not been checked, whatever the drafter believes. Keep one line per number: the figure, the source table, the denominator, the verdict, the date checked.

Do not ask the same AI that drafted the numbers to check them. That is not verification, it is a second opinion from the same source. The check must be independent of the tool that produced the figure — a human opening the source table, or a different tool with no access to the draft.

A worked example

Take the Skryvo claim again: "47% of email recipients open based on the subject line (Mailchimp)". Run the check:

  • Step 1: the figure is 47%; the claim is that nearly half of email recipients open an email because of its subject line, and that Mailchimp is the source.
  • Step 2: the source table would be a Mailchimp study or dataset reporting open rates by subject line. Searching for it finds no such table — the claim cannot be traced to any Mailchimp publication.
  • Step 3: there is no denominator to check, because there is no source table. The check stops here.
  • Verdict: invented. The number is precise, the attribution is a real company, and the figure has no home. Drop the sentence, or replace it with a figure you can trace.

Now the subtler version, which the citation guides do not catch. Suppose the same sentence cited a real Mailchimp report, and the report really does contain a 47% figure — but the table shows 47% of opened emails were opened within the first hour, and your sentence says 47% of recipients open because of the subject line. The citation resolves. The source is real. The digits match. The claim is still wrong: the number measures timing, not causation, and the denominator is opened emails, not all recipients. That is failure mode 2, and only step 3 catches it — because step 3 asks what the percentage is of before it asks whether the digits match.

What to do with a failed number

  • Invented: drop it, or replace it with a figure you can trace to a source table. Never keep a number you could not find, and never soften it into vagueness ("a large share") to keep the sentence — a vague claim built on an invented number is still built on nothing.
  • Wrong denominator, stale, or wrong population: re-source the claim. Either find the figure for the denominator, period and population your sentence actually describes, or rewrite the sentence to describe what the source actually measured. Do not quietly swap the citation — record the correction.
  • Transposed: correct the digits from the source table and re-run step 5. A transposition is the one failure where the fix is mechanical, but it still gets logged: the record is what proves the draft was checked.
  • Unverifiable: if the source is paywalled, offline, or otherwise unreachable, the number is unverifiable, not verified. Say so, or leave it out. An unverifiable number labelled as such is honest; an unverifiable number presented as sourced is not.

Common traps

  • A percentage with no base. "Adoption rose 40%." Forty percent of what, from what starting point? A rise from 5% to 7% is a 40% relative rise and a 2-percentage-point rise — the same data, two very different sentences.
  • "Up to" quoted as the average. The maximum in a range presented as the typical value. Check whether the source's headline figure is a mean, a median, or a maximum — AI drafts routinely promote the biggest number in the table.
  • A survey of a few hundred presented as a population. "n = 214" buried in the methodology, "Indian consumers" in your sentence. The population in the sentence must match the population in the sample.
  • Currency and year confusion. A figure in 2019 dollars quoted beside a 2025 figure with no adjustment and no date. Money without a year is not comparable to money with one.
  • A press-release number quoted as a study finding. Press releases round, select, and sometimes overstate. Trace past the release to the study or dataset it summarises; the release is a pointer, not a source.
  • A real number from a fabricated page. In October 2026, a researcher generated 50,000 fabricated statistics pages with an AI model in a single day, for under $10, and watched AI assistants cite them for months afterwards. The pages carried fabricated methodology statements, named institutional sources, review dates and research-team bylines — every trust signal, none of it real. The traffic and citation figures for that experiment are the author's own self-reported numbers, so treat the episode as an illustration of the failure shape, not as evidence of its scale. The lesson stands either way: a page that looks rigorous is not rigorous. Open the source table, or the number is unchecked.

A 10-minute pre-submission routine for numbers

  1. List every statistic in the draft — every percentage, every count, every figure with a unit. If the draft has more than twenty, list the load-bearing ones and sample the rest.
  2. For each one, write the denominator, the reference period, the population and the units. Any blank is a number that is not checked yet.
  3. Open the source table for each load-bearing number and compare the digits. Recompute at least one headline figure from the source data.
  4. Re-run step 2 on the day you submit: a number can be re-published, corrected or retracted while your draft is still in revision.
  5. Keep the log with the draft. When a reader asks where a number came from, the log is the answer. "We checked it" without a record is a claim, not evidence — and it is the same kind of claim this guide exists to catch.
