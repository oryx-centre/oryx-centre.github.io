DPDP Act and AI systems India

What India's DPDP Act, 2023 and the DPDP Rules, 2025 mean for AI systems: the duties, the dates, and the questions to ask a vendor.

In short: India’s DPDP Act, 2023 sets the duties for processing personal data in digital form — and AI systems process personal data constantly. This guide maps each duty onto the question to ask a vendor and the evidence to expect. It is a mapping explainer, not legal advice: it does not tell you whether your system complies, and it does not verify any vendor’s compliance claim.

India’s data protection law is the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (the DPDP Act). It sets the rules for processing personal data in digital form — and AI systems process personal data constantly: training data, prompts typed into a chatbot, interview transcripts fed to a research assistant, and the inferences an AI system draws about a person. If your organisation buys, builds, or deploys an AI system that touches personal data of people in India, the DPDP Act is part of the check — not because AI is named in it (it is not), but because the duties it sets fall on whoever decides why and how personal data is processed, whatever tool they use.

This guide explains the Act the way Oryx explains everything else: as claims that need evidence. Each duty below is stated, then translated into the question you would ask and the evidence you would expect to see. A reader who never contacts Oryx should still leave able to read a vendor’s “DPDP compliant” claim and check whether it points at anything real.

The Act in brief

The DPDP Act, 2023 (Act No. 22 of 2023) was enacted on 11 August 2023. It applies to the processing of digital personal data — personal data in digital form, or personal data collected in non-digital form and then digitised — within India. It also applies to processing outside India where it is connected with offering goods or services to people in India (Section 3). An AI system hosted abroad that serves Indian users is therefore in scope; being outside India does not take a vendor out of the Act.

Two exclusions matter. The Act does not apply to personal data that a person has made publicly available, or that has been made publicly available by someone else (Section 3(c)). “It was on the internet” is not the same as “it was made publicly available” — scraped data is not automatically excluded, and a vendor that claims it is should be asked to show why.

The Act sets the duties; the DPDP Rules, 2025 set out how and when they apply. The Rules were notified on 13 November 2025 (G.S.R. 846(E)), with a phased commencement:

DateWhat comes into forceWhat it means
13 November 2025Rules 1, 2, 17 to 21; the Board provisions of the ActDefinitions and the regulator’s set-up. No duties on ordinary businesses yet.
13 November 2026Rule 4 (Consent Managers); Act Section 6(9)Registration opens for Consent Managers — the intermediaries that let a person manage consent in one place. No new duties on ordinary businesses that day.
13 May 2027Rules 3, 5 to 16, 22 and 23; the substantive duties of the ActNotice, consent, security safeguards, breach notification, rights, retention, and penalties. This is the date to be ready for.

The Data Protection Board of India — the regulator that adjudicates breaches — has a live portal at dpbi.gov.in (live since 13 November 2026), but as of 15 November 2026 no Chairperson or Members had been appointed, and it was not yet taking complaints against Data Fiduciaries. Enforcement begins in earnest once the Board is seated. A challenge to the Act is before the Supreme Court (notice issued 27 October 2026); as of early November 2026 no stay had been granted. Check the current position before planning around any of this.

The roles: who owes what

The Act assigns duties by role, not by job title. The same organisation can be a fiduciary for its own data and a processor for someone else’s.

  • Data Principal — the individual the personal data is about. The person whose data an AI system processes.
  • Data Fiduciary — the person who, alone or with others, determines the purpose and means of processing personal data (Section 2(i)). If your organisation decides an AI system will process your users’ or employees’ personal data, your organisation is the fiduciary. The duties in this guide fall on the fiduciary.
  • Data Processor — the person who processes personal data on behalf of a Data Fiduciary (Section 2(k)). An AI vendor that processes your users’ data on your instructions is a processor. The fiduciary must have a valid contract with the processor (Section 8(2)), and the fiduciary remains responsible for the processing.
  • Consent Manager — a person registered with the Board who acts as a single point of contact for a Data Principal to give, manage, review, and withdraw consent (Section 6(7)–(9)). Using one is optional. A consent tool does not make a vendor a Consent Manager — only Board registration does, and registration opened on 13 November 2026.
  • Significant Data Fiduciary — a Data Fiduciary the government designates as significant, based on factors such as the volume and sensitivity of data processed and the risk to the rights of Data Principals (Section 10). Significant Data Fiduciaries carry additional duties: appointing a Data Protection Officer, appointing an independent data auditor, and conducting data protection impact assessments. The Act provides for designation; check the current Gazette notifications before assuming whether a vendor is one.

The duties that touch AI systems

Consent (Sections 5–6)

Personal data may be processed only with the Data Principal’s consent, or for one of the Act’s specified legitimate uses (Section 4). Consent must be free, specific, informed, unconditional, and unambiguous, given by a clear affirmative action (Section 6(1)). It must be accompanied by a notice stating the personal data to be processed, the purposes, and how the principal can exercise their rights and make a complaint (Section 5). Consent can be withdrawn at any time (Section 6(4)).

For AI systems, consent is where most claims fail. Three checks:

  • Specific purpose. Consent given for one purpose does not cover another. Data collected to answer a support query is not consented for training a model. A vendor that says “users consented” should be asked: consented to what, exactly, and where is the record?
  • The notice must be real. A notice buried in terms of service, or written so no user could understand what data the AI system processes, is not the notice Section 5 requires. Ask to see the actual notice shown to users.
  • Withdrawal must work. If consent is withdrawn, processing must stop and the data must be erased (Section 8(7)). Ask the vendor: when a user withdraws consent, what is deleted, from where, and how quickly?

Legitimate uses (Section 7). The Act allows processing without consent for a short list of specified purposes — including employment-related processing, compliance with law, medical emergencies, and sovereign functions. “We process it for AI training” is not on that list. A vendor relying on a legitimate use should be asked to name the one in Section 7 it relies on.

Purpose limitation and data minimisation

The Act does not use the phrase “data minimisation”, but its structure imposes it: processing must be for the purpose stated in the notice, and the fiduciary must erase personal data when the purpose is served or consent is withdrawn (Section 8(7)). Data kept “just in case” for future model training, after the stated purpose is served, is data held without a lawful basis.

For AI systems this bites twice. First, training data: a dataset collected for one purpose and then used to train a model is a new purpose, needing its own ground. Second, retention: prompts, logs, and conversation histories are personal data while they identify a person; keeping them indefinitely because storage is cheap is not a purpose.

The question to ask. For each dataset the system touches: what was the stated purpose, when does it expire, and what is deleted when it does?

Evidence to expect. A data inventory — what personal data the system processes, for which purpose, under which ground, and for how long — with a deletion rule attached to each entry. The Rules also require certain records, including consent records and breach logs, to be kept for at least one year. A vendor with no inventory cannot show it processes data lawfully, whatever its marketing says.

Security safeguards and breach notification (Section 8)

The Data Fiduciary must take reasonable security safeguards to prevent a personal data breach (Section 8(5)). On a breach, it must notify the Data Protection Board and each affected Data Principal without delay (Section 8(6)); the Rules set a 72-hour window for notifying the Board.

For AI systems, the breach surface is wider than a database leak. Prompt-injection attacks that exfiltrate data, a model that regurgitates training data containing personal data, and an API that returns another user’s records are all personal data breaches if personal data is exposed. A vendor’s security claim should cover the AI-specific surface, not just the database.

The question to ask. What happens when the system leaks personal data — who is told, how fast, and what is the record?

Evidence to expect. A breach response procedure with named roles and a clock (the 72-hour Board notification), and evidence of security testing that includes the AI layer — not only network and database controls. The maximum penalty in the Act’s Schedule is &rupee;250 crore, and the Schedule sets different caps for different breaches; the figure is a maximum, not a tariff, and penalties can only be imposed once the Board is seated and the relevant provisions are in force.

Children’s data (Section 9)

A child is a person below 18 years of age. A Data Fiduciary must obtain verifiable parental consent before processing a child’s personal data, must not undertake tracking or behavioural monitoring of children, must not direct targeted advertising at children, and must not process a child’s data in a way likely to cause any detrimental effect.

This is the duty most likely to catch AI products by surprise. An AI tutoring product, a homework helper, or a learning app that tracks a child’s progress, builds a profile of their weaknesses, or personalises content from their behaviour is doing behavioural monitoring of a child — which Section 9 prohibits outright, even with parental consent. Consent does not cure a prohibition.

The question to ask. Does this system process any child’s data, and if so, where is the verifiable parental consent — and what tracking, monitoring, or targeting has been switched off?

Evidence to expect. An age gate, a parental-consent flow that can be shown (not merely asserted), and a written statement of which features are disabled for children. A vendor that cannot show the parental-consent flow has not obtained verifiable consent.

Rights of Data Principals (Sections 11–14)

A Data Principal has the right to obtain information about the processing of their personal data, to seek correction, completion, and updating of it, to seek erasure, to seek grievance redressal, and to nominate another person to exercise these rights in the event of death or incapacity. The fiduciary must publish the means to exercise these rights and the contact details of a person who can answer questions (Section 8(3), (4)).

For AI systems, two honest limits matter:

  • Correction and erasure apply to the data held. If an AI system stores a wrong fact about a person in its database, the person can demand it be corrected or erased. The Act’s erasure right is about the data the fiduciary holds — it does not, on its face, require a trained model to be retrained. What erasure means for a model that has already been trained on the data is not settled in the Act. A vendor that promises “full erasure including from the model” should be asked how, exactly, that is done; a vendor that says “erasure is impossible because the model is trained” should be asked what it does instead.
  • Inferences are personal data too. An output that says something about a person — a risk score, a classification, a summary of their behaviour — is personal data about that person. A system that infers things about people cannot claim it processes no personal data merely because it collected none directly.

The question to ask. When a person asks for their data to be corrected or erased, what changes, where, and how do you prove it?

Evidence to expect. A rights-request procedure with a response time, a record of requests handled, and a written answer to the trained-model question. A vendor with no procedure for rights requests has no way to honour them.

Transfers outside India (Section 16)

The Act allows the transfer of personal data to other countries, except to countries the government may notify as restricted. No such restricted list had been published as of this guide’s writing — check the current position before relying on it.

This matters because AI systems routinely process data outside India: the model may run on servers abroad, the vendor’s support team may be elsewhere, and sub-processors may be in several countries. The fiduciary remains responsible for the processing wherever it happens.

The question to ask. In which countries is this personal data processed, by whom, and under what contract?

Evidence to expect. A list of sub-processors and their locations, and the contracts that bind them. A vendor that cannot name its sub-processors cannot show the fiduciary where the data goes.

A worked example: mapping one system

Take an AI research assistant sold to an Indian university. Researchers upload interview transcripts — personal data of the interviewees — and the assistant summarises themes, extracts quotes, and answers questions about the interviews. Map it against the duties:

  1. Consent and notice (Sections 5–6): Did the interviewees consent to their transcripts being processed by an AI system, or only to being interviewed? Was the notice given to them before consent, and does it name the AI processing? If the consent was for the interview only, the AI processing has no ground.
  2. Purpose limitation: The transcripts were collected for one research project. If the vendor also uses them to train its models, that is a new purpose — it needs its own consent. Ask: are uploaded transcripts used for training? The answer must be in writing.
  3. Data minimisation and retention: Transcripts contain names, places, and identifying details. Are they minimised before upload, or does the system store the full transcript? When is it deleted — at the end of the project, or never? Ask for the deletion rule.
  4. Security and breach (Section 8): What safeguards protect the transcripts, and what happens if the model reveals one interviewee’s answers to another researcher? Ask for the breach procedure and the 72-hour Board notification.
  5. Rights (Sections 11–14): An interviewee asks for their transcript to be erased. What is deleted — the stored transcript, the summaries, the model’s answers that quote them? Ask for the written answer to the trained-model question.
  6. Processor contract (Section 8(2)): The university is the fiduciary; the vendor is the processor. Is there a valid contract limiting the vendor to the university’s instructions? Ask to see its terms.
  7. Children’s data (Section 9): If any interviewees are under 18, verifiable parental consent is required and behavioural monitoring is prohibited. Ask whether the system is used with minors at all.
  8. Transfers (Section 16): Where does the model run, and where are the transcripts stored? Ask for the sub-processor list.

One system, eight questions, eight pieces of evidence. A vendor that can answer all eight has done the work. A vendor that can answer none of them is selling a claim, not a compliant system.

Questions to send a vendor (copy-paste list)

  1. What personal data does this system process, for which stated purpose, and under which ground — consent or a specified legitimate use under Section 7?
  2. May we see the notice given to Data Principals before consent was taken?
  3. Are uploaded or collected personal data used to train models? If so, on what ground, and can we opt out in writing?
  4. What is the retention and deletion rule for personal data — including prompts, logs, and conversation history — and how is deletion verified?
  5. When a Data Principal asks for correction or erasure, what changes, where, and how do you handle data already used to train a model?
  6. Does the system process any child’s data? If so, show us the verifiable parental-consent flow and confirm that tracking, behavioural monitoring, and targeted advertising are switched off for children.
  7. What is your breach procedure — who is notified, within what time, and what evidence do we receive? What AI-specific testing (prompt injection, data exfiltration) has been done?
  8. Who are your sub-processors, in which countries is personal data processed, and may we see the processor contract that limits processing to our instructions?

Common mistakes

  • Assuming the Act does not reach you. The Act applies to processing outside India connected with offering goods or services to people in India. A foreign vendor serving Indian users is in scope.
  • Treating the 13 May 2027 date as a reason to wait. The substantive duties start then, but the work — inventory, notices, consent flows, deletion rules, processor contracts — takes months. Organisations that start in May 2027 will be late.
  • Confusing the Board’s portal with enforcement. The Board’s portal is live, but as of mid-November 2026 no Chairperson or Members had been appointed. A live website is not a seated regulator, and penalties cannot be imposed until the Board is seated and the provisions are in force.
  • Claiming compliance from a vendor’s slide. “DPDP compliant” on a sales deck is a claim. The evidence is the notice, the consent records, the data inventory, the deletion rules, the breach procedure, and the processor contract. Ask for the evidence, not the adjective.
  • Forgetting the children’s rules in EdTech AI. Parental consent does not cure the prohibitions on tracking, behavioural monitoring, and targeted advertising directed at children. A product that profiles children is prohibited from doing so, whatever its consent flow says.
  • Assuming erasure means retraining. The Act’s erasure right covers the data held. Whether it requires retraining a model is not settled in the Act — and a vendor that claims either answer with certainty should be asked to show its reasoning.
  • Assuming consent covers any later use. Consent is for a specific purpose. Reusing consented data to train a model is a new purpose, and it needs its own ground.

What this guide cannot do

This guide maps the Act’s duties onto the questions and evidence a reader can check. It does not determine whether a particular system complies — that determination needs the system’s actual data flows, its notices, and its contracts, applied by someone who knows the facts. It does not verify any vendor’s compliance claim; only the notice, the consent records, the inventory, and the contracts do that. It does not replace a qualified adviser’s opinion on an organisation’s obligations, and it does not address sector-specific law (such as the IT Act, or the SPDI Rules where they still apply), state rules, or the standards that may support compliance. If a decision carries legal risk, consult a qualified adviser and read the Act itself on India Code and the Rules as notified in the Gazette.
